Blunt read: over the next week, Iraq is unlikely to explode into a new domestic civil crisis. The country is going to be consumed by three overlapping problems at once: the fallout from the drone attack on Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline, the final stages of the U.S. military exit, and Baghdad’s attempt to turn regional chaos into an oil-export opportunity through Turkey, Europe, and discounted crude sales.

I would expect a week of heavy diplomacy, energy logistics, militia posturing, and managed tension — not necessarily a full-blown war inside Iraq, but a lot of pressure on Baghdad to prove it can still control the narrative.

Most likely base case for Iraq in the next 7 days
1. Baghdad will try very hard to contain the Saudi pipeline attack fallout
This is probably the biggest domestic political issue facing Iraq right now. The attack apparently originated from Iraqi territory, and that gives Riyadh a strong argument that Baghdad does not fully control its armed factions.

I expect the following:

The Iraqi government will announce more details of its investigation into the September 11 drone strike on Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline.
It will almost certainly blame a “resistance splinter,” a PMF faction, or an armed group operating outside the official chain of command.
Baghdad will avoid naming the most powerful militia leaders directly, because it cannot afford to make them all enemies at once.
There may be some lower-level detentions, checkpoints, or security sweeps in Anbar, western Iraq, and possibly Basra province to show Riyadh that something is being done.
Iraqi-Saudi backchannel contacts will intensify. I would not expect a formal public reconciliation next week, but I would expect quiet coordination.
Baghdad will try to frame the incident as an isolated act by a faction, not proof that Iraq has become a permanent launching pad for attacks on its neighbors.
The political trick here is simple: Baghdad must look strong enough to discipline militias, but weak enough in practice that the militias do not revolt.

2. The U.S. withdrawal will move into its final visible phase
The headlines say the last U.S. troops are packing up and that major deadlines hit at the end of September. That means the next week is going to be full of exit logistics, symbolism, and militia signaling.

I expect:

Final drawdown activity in northern Iraq, especially around Kurdish areas where U.S. forces have been concentrated.
More visible movement of equipment, vehicles, and personnel toward Erbil or other exit points.
Iraqi government and KRG officials issuing coordinated statements about “handover,” “transition,” or “completion” of the mission.
Possible small technical delays. Even if the political deadline is end of September, some air-defense, logistics, or advisory personnel may remain for a short bridge period. Baghdad will probably call that a “technical extension,” not a reversal.
PMF and Iran-linked militias issuing strong statements saying their weapons are staying. That is now almost a ritual: they will frame the U.S. departure as a victory without going so far as to trigger an all-out confrontation with Baghdad or the KRG.
I would not expect a major battle inside Iraq next week unless there is a surprise Iranian or Houthi escalation. But I would expect drone alerts, air-defense activity, and at least one or two tense moments around bases or corridors.

3. The Iraqi PM’s U.S./UN trip will dominate diplomacy
The headline that the Iraqi prime minister will visit the United States next week, attend UN General Assembly events, and meet President Trump is very important. This trip will probably be Baghdad’s main diplomatic offensive for the week.

I expect Zaidi to use the trip to do four things:

Tell Washington that Iraq is not a front line in the U.S.-Iran war and does not want to become one.
Ask for security guarantees or at least political reassurance that the U.S. will still protect Iraqi interests after the main military presence leaves.
Push European partners, especially France and Germany, into deeper energy and security roles.
Sell Iraq as a stable oil-export alternative while Hormuz is disrupted and Saudi Arabia’s pipeline problem is being fixed.
The likely outcome is not a grand treaty next week. It will be more like:

joint statements,
memorandums of understanding,
technical working groups,
energy investment talks,
and public photo opportunities showing Iraq as “pivoting” to Europe while still keeping Washington close.
France and Germany are especially important here because Baghdad is trying to build a post-U.S. security and energy partnership with Europe. I would expect some kind of French-Iraqi or German-Iraqi energy/security statement to come out during or immediately after the trip.

4. Oil exports will become Iraq’s main economic weapon
This is where things get interesting. Iraq is in a relatively strong position right now because:

Hormuz is disrupted or at least highly risky,
Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline has been hit,
global oil prices are elevated,
Europe and Asia need alternative supply,
Iraq can move crude through Turkey, discounted sales, and new logistics routes.
I expect Baghdad to push hard on several fronts next week:

a. OPEC quota pressure
Iraq has formally asked OPEC for a 6 million bpd quota. That is far above its current ceiling. I would not be surprised if Baghdad gets sympathy, but I would not expect a full formal OPEC+ approval within just seven days unless there is an emergency meeting.

More likely:

Iraq will lobby hard at the UN and in bilateral talks.
There may be an informal OPEC+ discussion or special committee review.
Baghdad may get a temporary increase, a waiver, or permission to sell extra barrels outside the normal framework because of regional disruption.
If no formal decision comes next week, Iraq will still announce “preparatory” steps for higher exports.
b. Trucking and northern export routes
The pilot operation moving southern crude by road to Kirkuk is a big signal. It means Iraq is trying to bypass southern port bottlenecks and push more oil north through Turkey.

I expect:

expansion of the trucking pilot,
new logistics announcements with Turkish or regional operators,
more focus on TCPL/Kirkuk-Turkey flows,
attempts to reduce friction between Baghdad and Erbil over export revenues and routing.
This is not a perfect system. Road transport is expensive, security is complicated, and KRG-Baghdad revenue disputes are never far away. But politically, it looks like action.

c. ADNOC, TotalEnergies, and European buyers
The ADNOC purchase of discounted Iraqi crude and the start of talks with TotalEnergies fit the same pattern: Iraq is trying to lock in buyers before its export situation becomes even more complicated.

I would expect more announcements like:

new term sheets,
framework agreements,
energy project discussions,
European interest in Iraqi gas, power, and refinery upgrades,
possibly some French or German state-backed investment talks tied to the PM’s diplomatic tour.
The message Baghdad wants to send is: “Even if the U.S. military leaves, Iraq’s oil economy is becoming more diversified.”

5. Border crossings with Iran will likely see phased reopening
Iraq has already set a phased reopening schedule for border crossings with Iran after security and technical reviews. I would expect that process to move forward next week, at least partially.

Why?

It helps trade.
It reduces smuggling pressure.
It gives Baghdad some control over movement.
It also lets Iraq claim it is managing its borders rather than letting militias and smugglers run them.
I would expect:

one or more crossings to reopen in a limited or phased way,
new security protocols announced,
Iraqi officials claiming the move is based on “security readiness,”
and continued tension with PMF factions that may see border control as their own turf.
This is also important because if the U.S.-Iran war continues, Iraq’s western and southern borders become even more sensitive. Baghdad wants to show it can open controlled gateways without becoming a weak point.

6. Civilian aviation will keep expanding, but with military caveats
Iraq has lifted decade-old restrictions on western airspace for civilian aviation. That is a meaningful sovereignty and commercial signal.

I expect:

more civilian flights using Iraqi airspace,
possible new routes or charter operations,
airlines testing longer-haul paths through Iraq,
and continued temporary military closures during missile/drone alerts if the regional war escalates.
This is a small but important domestic win for Baghdad. It shows that normalcy is returning even while the country is still surrounded by conflict.

What I think will be the main internal political struggle
The real fight inside Iraq next week will be between:

Baghdad’s need to look sovereign
Opening airspace, reopening borders, signing European deals, pushing OPEC quotas, and managing U.S. exit all serve this purpose.
The PMF’s need to keep leverage
If the U.S. leaves and Baghdad gets closer to Europe, militias will want to make sure they are not sidelined. They may push for:
a role in border security,
influence over Iran-linked trade,
control of certain export corridors,
and protection from being blamed for every regional attack.
Kurdistan’s interest in exports
The KRG will want to make sure northern oil flows continue smoothly and that it gets its share of revenue. If U.S. forces are leaving northern bases, Erbil and Baghdad will need to coordinate on security and export logistics. That can work, but it can also produce friction.
I do not expect this to become a full Baghdad-Erbil crisis next week unless one of two things happens:

the U.S. exit gets messy, or
there is a new militia attack that makes both sides blame each other.

What I would not expect next week, unless something big happens
I would be surprised if we saw:

a full Iraqi government collapse,
a PM resignation crisis,
a all-out PMF takeover of Baghdad,
a new formal U.S.-Iran ceasefire announced from Iraq,
a major OPEC+ decision on Iraq’s 6 million bpd quota in just seven days,
or a large-scale ground war involving Iraqi territory.
That is not to say none of these are possible. The region is unstable enough that surprises can happen fast. But the center of gravity right now is oil, diplomacy, and exit management.

Alternative scenarios
Scenario A: Managed tension continues — most likely
This is my base case.

What happens:

U.S. withdrawal moves forward with some symbolic delays.
Iraq blames a militia splinter for the Saudi pipeline attack.
Baghdad signs energy/diplomatic statements with Europe and the U.S.
Oil export workarounds expand.
Border and airspace openings continue in phased form.
Probability: 55–60%

Scenario B: Diplomatic de-escalation improves Iraq’s position
This would be a positive surprise.

What happens:

Oman, Saudi Arabia, Iran, and other regional players resume talks.
A temporary Hormuz or pipeline arrangement is announced.
Oil prices cool further.
Iraq gets more support for a higher OPEC quota.
Baghdad uses the moment to announce bigger European energy deals.
Probability: 20–25%

Scenario C: Militia escalation shakes Iraq harder
This is the dangerous one.

What happens:

A PMF faction or “resistance splinter” attacks a Saudi target, a U.S.-linked site in the north, or another Gulf energy facility from Iraqi territory.
Riyadh demands stronger Iraqi action.
The U.S. conducts limited strikes or asks for use of Iraqi/Kurdish airspace.
Baghdad is forced to choose between public discipline and quiet tolerance.
Oil prices spike again.
Probability: 15–20%

Bottom line
Iraq next week will probably look like a country trying to turn crisis into opportunity.

Baghdad’s goal is simple:

keep the U.S. departing without chaos,
keep the militias armed but not fully out of control,
keep Europe and Gulf buyers interested in Iraqi oil,
and keep the Saudi pipeline attack from becoming a long-term regional liability.
If I had to sum it up in one sentence:

Expect Iraq to spend the next week doing diplomacy, moving oil north, blaming a militia splinter, and letting the U.S. leave while Baghdad tries to prove it can still be the region’s energy hub.
