Hello :-) I read the headlines as a set of “tea leaves,” not as a full newsroom picture, but the pattern is pretty clear.

Assuming you mean the next week from September 22 to September 29, 2026, my short prediction is:

South Korea will have a “pressure management” week, not a war week.
President Lee Jae Myung will be abroad at the UN and in Mexico, trying to turn his approval-rating nosedive into a diplomatic reset. North Korea will keep applying pressure with missiles and new weapon claims. Seoul will try to show economic strength through chips, AI, minerals, energy, and exports while domestic politics gets noisy around housing, cabinet nominations, the Seoul mayor, abortion pills, and the DMZ incident.

Here is my forecast without tables.

1. President Lee’s UN/Mexico trip becomes the main diplomatic story
Likelihood: high

The biggest scheduled event is Lee Jae Myung’s visit to the United States and Mexico from September 21 to 27 for the UN General Assembly and the Sheinbaum summit. That means next week will be dominated by his statements, side meetings, and whatever announcements come out of those contacts.

I would expect him to use the trip for three things:

Reset his domestic image.
His approval rating has nosedived, so the government needs the UN week to look like a success tour: diplomacy, investment, minerals, AI, energy, nuclear cooperation, and exports.
Push back on Middle East pressure while keeping the US alliance warm.
He has already ruled out deploying military assets in a way that could drag South Korea into the Middle East conflict. Next week he will probably repeat that in a more polished international form: “We are a reliable ally, but we will not be automatically pulled into every regional war.”
Sell the $350 billion US investment pledge as strategic, not painful.
He has said the US investment talks are causing him “sleepless nights,” so next week he will likely try to frame the $350 billion package as a long-term deal for chips, AI, energy, defense industry, and Korean corporate interests. Expect language about “shared growth,” “Korea-US investment arm,” bonds, critical minerals, and supply chains.
I would bet on at least one of these happening:

A Lee-Trump or Lee-Rubio side meeting or phone call.
A Korea-Mexico joint statement on trade, energy, critical minerals, AI, tourism, or investment.
Announcements following up the Central Asia summit, especially around Kazakhstan nuclear cooperation, mineral trade, uranium, rare earths, or energy security.
More public emphasis on South Korea’s role in global AI and semiconductor supply chains.
The opposition will probably attack him for “giving too much to Washington” or “making promises that burden Korean taxpayers,” while his camp will say the trip proves Korea is a serious global player again.

2. North Korea will likely launch another missile or show off a new weapon
Likelihood: high, maybe 60–75%

North Korea has already launched two short-range ballistic missiles toward the sea, and it is touting a new hypersonic weapons system. Given that pattern, I would expect Pyongyang to keep testing during Lee’s UN week.

Most likely next-week North Korean actions:

Another short-range ballistic missile launch toward the Sea of Japan/East Sea.
A claim about a new “hypersonic” or “ultra-modern” weapon system.
A propaganda statement blaming the US for regional tension.
Possibly an artillery or coastal missile demonstration if Seoul/US hold any military exercises or public statements that Pyongyang can use as an excuse.
What I would not expect unless something changes suddenly:

A full ICBM salvo.
A direct attack on South Korea.
A major North-South exchange of fire.
A sudden collapse of the no-talks posture, unless Trump wants a photo-op summit announcement at the UN.
The Korean military will probably hold a JCS meeting, issue statements about tracking the missiles, and call for restraint. The political reaction in Seoul will be: “North Korea is testing US and South Korean patience while Lee is abroad.”

3. The DMZ explosion becomes a small but persistent political incident
Likelihood: high

The landmine or explosion that injured three South Korean military officers inside the DMZ is not likely to become a major war, but it will stay in the news because it gives the opposition an easy target.

I expect next week to include:

A defense ministry investigation statement.
More detail on whether it was a live landmine, an old mine, or some other explosive device.
Opposition questions about military readiness, border management, and why officers were injured.
Possibly increased patrols, demining work, or safety measures in the DMZ.
North Korea either staying quiet or making a propaganda statement blaming Seoul or claiming the incident was accidental.
This will not dominate the whole week, but it will be used domestically to criticize Lee’s government and the military establishment. It is the kind of story that stays alive because it is simple: “Our soldiers were hurt on our border.”

4. Lee’s government will try to repair its standing with reshuffle news and policy announcements
Likelihood: high to medium-high

The headlines show a government under pressure: approval rating nosedive, ministerial nominee withdrawal, US investment stress, DMZ injury, North Korean missile tests, and market volatility. That means the Blue House will want to look busy and in control.

I would expect one or more of these next week:

A new justice minister nominee after the latest withdrawal.
More cabinet or senior official statements on housing supply.
A government push on cost-of-living, energy security, or semiconductor/AI investment.
A “reset” narrative around Lee’s leadership: less crisis talk, more future-facing policy language.
More emphasis on exports, chip demand, AI progress, and foreign investment as proof the economy is still strong.
The housing story is especially important. The nominee who pledged to speed up housing supply will probably be pushed forward because housing prices, Seoul real estate, and young voters’ frustration are major domestic pain points. If the government announces even a modest package on land, construction, or supply, it will try to turn it into a political win.

5. The Seoul mayor versus president fight over green space will become more visible
Likelihood: medium

The Reuters piece about Seoul’s green space being “a battleground for the mayor and South Korea’s president” suggests this is becoming a symbolic local-national political fight. I would not expect it to cause a constitutional crisis, but it will probably produce more public statements, press conferences, or policy clashes.

Next week could bring:

Mayor Oh Se-hoon defending Seoul’s urban greening, park, or development plans.
The presidential camp accusing the mayor of obstruction, delay, or political posturing.
The mayor accusing the central government of overreach or poor coordination.
A small public event around trees, parks, green corridors, or Seoul urban planning that becomes a political photo-op.
Opposition parties using it to show disunity inside the ruling camp or between Seoul and the national government.
This is not likely to be the biggest story, but it will keep appearing because it is local, visual, and easy for voters to understand: “The mayor and president are fighting over Seoul’s green space.”

6. Markets will stay volatile, with chips, AI, oil, US yields, and the $350 billion pledge driving swings
Likelihood: high for volatility

South Korea’s markets are going to be jumpy next week. The headlines already show several forces pulling in different directions:

Record early September exports on the chip boom.
AI investment optimism.
Chip stocks falling after AI leaders talk about slowing development.
US Treasury yields above 5%.
Oil prices rising because of Iran/Middle East tensions and Saudi pipeline concerns.
South Korea being exposed to crude supply shocks.
The $350 billion Korea-US investment pledge causing political stress.
A possible debut bond or framework from the Korea-US investment arm.
The Bank of Korea testing a 24-hour won settlement system.
My prediction for markets next week:

KOSPI will swing sharply, but I would not bet on a crash unless there is a sudden Iran/oil shock or US-China trade reversal.

More likely market stories:

“KOSPI swings as AI/chip stocks react to global tech news.”
“Won settlement system trial update draws foreign investor interest.”
“Korea-US investment arm moves closer to debut bond for $350 billion pledge.”
“Oil prices pressure South Korea’s energy import bill.”
“SK Hynix, Samsung, Intel, or KKR-related AI/chip supply chain deal headlines.”
There is a decent chance of at least one major corporate headline involving SK Hynix, Samsung, Intel, KKR, or another AI/semiconductor supply-chain player. The reason is that the headlines already show multiple threads: university-to-chipmaker talent pipelines, KKR’s $3 billion Korea deals, Hahn & Co real estate expansion, Intel-SK Hynix US manufacturing talks, and strong export momentum.

The government will probably try to use strong chip exports as evidence that the economy is fine even if Lee’s approval rating is not.

7. The Asian Games in Japan will keep producing sports headlines and minor diplomatic irritations
Likelihood: medium-high

The Asian Games are already producing embarrassment for organizers: wrong anthem, container housing complaints, transport issues, and logistics problems. South Korea’s delegation has been visibly unhappy about conditions. Next week will likely include more of that.

I expect:

South Korea pushing hard in medal events, especially football, hockey, basketball, boxing, judo, or other major team/individual sports.
More complaints from the Korean delegation about accommodation, transport, or organizer handling.
Japanese organizers issuing more apologies or explanations.
Social media memes about the anthem mix-up and housing issues.
A minor Seoul-Tokyo diplomatic irritation, probably handled quietly but visible in sports pages.
This will not be a foreign-policy crisis, but it will keep Korean readers engaged because sports, national pride, and “why is this happening to our athletes?” stories travel well.

If the Games close during this period, the final medal count and closing ceremony will also generate headlines. South Korea will probably want to frame its performance as strong despite organizer problems.

8. Domestic social and legal stories will stay active but below the main political line
Likelihood: medium to low for top-news dominance

Several smaller stories will keep running:

Abortion pills
South Korea is moving toward approving abortion pills for the first time next year. Next week may bring more technical or medical details, hospital association statements, conservative criticism, feminist support, and debate over safety, access, and timing. This is not going to be the biggest story, but it will remain a cultural-political flashpoint.

Former first lady’s reduced sentence
The appeals court reducing the sentence of the wife of ousted President Yoon Suk Yeol will keep generating political commentary. Progressive media may criticize the court as too lenient; conservative media may say the case was politically motivated. It will be used by both sides, but it is unlikely to become a major crisis unless there is a sudden new legal development.

Jeju missing-persons scandal
The AFP fact-check about fabricated graphics showing Jeju as a crime hotbed suggests social media is still amplifying the issue. I would expect more police or prosecutorial explanations, maybe an internal review, and continued online debate. It will probably stay a regional/national trust story rather than a top political headline.

Korean workers challenging the US over the Georgia raid
The 300-plus South Korean workers filing claims after the Hyundai-linked Georgia immigration raid will become a minor but useful irritant in US-Korea relations, especially while Lee is in New York. Seoul may raise it quietly with Washington because it touches on investment climate, worker protection, and Trump administration pressure. I would not expect a huge public fight unless the US makes a symbolic move against Korean workers or companies.
