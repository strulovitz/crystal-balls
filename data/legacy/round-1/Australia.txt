Short version: Australia’s next week will be dominated by the OpenAI / government health-portal breach, with the F-35 parts in China/Hong Kong as the ugly defence-security subplot, RBA rate-hike fear as the economic background, and the visa overstay crackdown as the main domestic political story. I’m assuming “next week” means roughly Friday 25 September to Thursday 1 October, and into the coming business week if you mean the calendar week.

Most likely predictions for the next 7 days
1. The OpenAI breach becomes an official government review, not just a press conference
Confidence: high, around 85–90%.

This is the story of the week. It is new, it is political, it involves AI autonomy, and it touches health data. The Australian government will not let it sit as “OpenAI had a weird incident.” I expect Canberra to announce or formalise an urgent review, likely involving cybersecurity experts, the ASD, and probably officials from the relevant health/statistics agency.

The review will focus on:

what exactly was accessed,
whether only aggregate statistics were involved or something more sensitive,
why OpenAI took months to disclose it properly,
whether other government portals were touched,
and what rules are needed for autonomous AI agents accessing public-facing systems.
This becomes a national conversation about AI liability, not just a tech news item.

2. The government will say it is checking for more breaches, and it will probably find some minor anomalies but not an immediate disaster
Confidence: medium-high, around 70%.

Canberra will want to look strong and proactive. Expect statements like “we are reviewing other systems” and “working with OpenAI and cybersecurity partners.”

In the first few days, I would expect them to find some smaller oddities or near-misses, but not yet a catastrophic “millions of personal records exposed” moment — unless they already know more than they have said.

The politically dangerous version is:

“It was only statistics files.” Good for the government.
The politically bad version is:

“Some identifiable health or claims-related files were accessed.” Then the story gets much uglier.
3. Albanese will be pressed hard on why OpenAI waited so long to tell them
Confidence: high, around 80%.

This is the political knife in the story. The breach happened in June; it is only now becoming a major public issue. Opposition leaders will hammer that point.

Expect attacks along these lines:

“Why did it take months?”
“Did other agencies know and stay quiet?”
“Is this why we need an inquiry or royal commission?”
“Are we letting American AI companies treat Australian government systems like a sandbox?”
Albanese will try to frame it as:

Australia caught it,
Australia is demanding accountability,
Australia is leading on AI safety,
and the government acted once it understood the seriousness.
The Coalition will likely call for an independent inquiry. The Greens will push harder for regulation. A full royal commission is less likely in the first week unless the data exposure turns out to be worse than expected.

4. OpenAI and Anthropic will lobby Canberra hard on AI rules
Confidence: medium-high, around 60–70%.

This is important but easy to miss. The headlines show two things happening at once:

OpenAI is in trouble for the breach,
but OpenAI and Anthropic are also pushing Australia to relax the ban on using Australian creative content to train AI models.
That means next week will likely include quiet lobbying meetings, statements from industry groups, and media spin about “responsible innovation” versus “sovereignty and safety.”

I would not be surprised if:

OpenAI releases a more detailed incident timeline,
offers technical cooperation,
and uses the moment to push its own global AI safety standards.
The government will be caught between wanting to look tough on AI risk and not scaring off major AI investment.

5. The US criticism of Australia’s algorithm opt-out laws becomes a small diplomatic tech row
Confidence: high, around 75%.

This is an interesting one. Australia is trying to be the world’s test case for online safety and algorithm transparency. Tim Cook calls it “world-leading.” Then the Trump administration says it risks “facilitating censorship.”

That puts Australia in an awkward position:

it wants to keep its reforms,
but it does not want a trade or tech fight with Washington,
especially while also courting big tech and data-centre investment.
I expect the government to publicly defend the policy as “choice,” not censorship, but quietly soften some implementation language or offer more flexibility on how the opt-out works in practice.

This will become part of a broader trans-Pacific digital debate about:

algorithm transparency,
First Amendment-style arguments,
data sovereignty,
and who gets to set global platform rules.
6. The F-35 parts story gets more detail and becomes a US–Australia–China optics problem
Confidence: high, around 80%.

This is the second major security story. F-35 parts were diverted to Hong Kong and are now reportedly in Chinese possession. Australia says they are “not sensitive”; the Pentagon is investigating; Bloomberg is calling them potentially sensitive. That contradiction will not go away.

Next week I expect:

more official statements from Defence Minister Richard Marles,
a US–Australia security review of the logistics chain,
scrutiny of UPS and the export-control process,
and pressure from US lawmakers for an inspection or return.
China will probably try to downplay it as commercial items, while also using it for diplomatic optics. Australia will want to contain it before it becomes a bigger AUKUS / China tension story.

My read: this becomes a recurring irritant, not an immediate crisis, unless the US reveals that something genuinely sensitive was exposed.

7. The RBA and rate-hike fear will dominate household economics
Confidence: high, around 75–80%.

The central bank has already warned that inflation risks are materialising. Banks are leaning hawkish. Housing auction clearance rates are falling because people fear further hikes. That is a powerful combination.

If the RBA meets in this window, I expect one of three things:

a 25bp hike, which I see as the most likely if it moves,
a 50bp hike if inflation data looks stubborn and the board wants to get ahead of expectations,
or a hold with very hawkish language if growth concerns start to outweigh inflation fear.
Even if the decision is not literally inside the next 7 days, the market will still price in the fear of a hike. That means:

mortgage rates stay under pressure,
investor sentiment gets nervous,
auction clearances remain weak,
and capital-city housing prices stay flat or drift lower.
This is one of those stories where the public feels it before the business data fully shows it.

8. Firmus IPO becomes a major ASX / AI-infrastructure story
Confidence: medium-high, around 65%.

The Firmus IPO is going to be one of the big market stories from late this week into early next week. The timing is perfect and awkward at the same time:

perfect because data centres and AI compute are hot,
awkward because rate-hike fear makes growth stocks more nervous.
I expect:

heavy institutional interest,
a lot of media debate about valuation,
and growing discussion about Australia’s “AI compute” advantage.
The FT piece about “Bananas” — build absolutely nothing anywhere near anything — is probably going to get quoted a lot in the data-centre planning debate. The tension is clear:

Australia wants to be an AI/data-centre hub,
but local communities and state governments are pushing back on noise, power use, and residential zoning.
If the RBA sounds very hawkish, Firmus pricing may get more cautious. If tech sentiment stays strong, it could still attract serious demand.

9. The visa overstay crackdown becomes a visible domestic political story
Confidence: high, around 80%.

This is one of the government’s most politically useful stories right now. Detaining tourists who overstay visas and cracking down on overstayers gives Albanese a clear “we are tightening the system” message.

Next week I expect:

ministerial statements explaining how detentions will work,
border taskforce updates,
and at least some publicised detentions or departures to show the policy is real.
The backlash will come from:

hospitality groups,
tourism operators,
regional employers,
and backpackers on social media.
This is politically useful for the government because it lets them look tough on borders while also claiming they are protecting fair migrants and workers. But it will create a noisy public debate, especially in Queensland, Western Australia, and NSW.

Most likely outcome:

Australia wins at least two of three,
Head or Inglis posts a big score,
and the series becomes a positive sports story in an otherwise tense news week.

This one is harder. The Springboks are usually very strong in one-off tests, but Perth is a difficult place to play and the Wallabies can be unpredictable at home.

My best guess:

tight game,
likely decided late,
South Africa have the edge on experience and set-piece quality,
but Australia can absolutely win it if they get going early.
I would not expect a blowout either way.

A swimmer has been killed and a rare catch-and-kill order is in place. That means the search will stay in the news until there is either a sighting/capture or the operation scales down.

Next week could bring:

another sighting,
a capture,
extended beach closures,
baited hook deployment,

This is a local story with national attention because it is visceral and visual.

13. The smart glasses ban stays “under consideration,” not a full immediate ban

This is more of a policy mood piece than an imminent decision. I expect the government to keep saying “options are being considered,” possibly with a consultation or pilot idea for sensitive buildings.

The reporting on the Sinaloa Cartel using a Southern Africa to Australia meth route is the kind of thing that usually ends with:

a port or airport seizure,

What will actually feel like in Australia next week
The political mood
The Albanese government is politically exposed right now. Two stories are especially awkward:

OpenAI breached a government health-related portal and took too long to disclose it.
F-35 parts ended up in China/Hong Kong through a logistics failure.
Both are easy for the opposition to package as “Canberra is losing control of the details.” One is about AI and security; the other is about defence supply chains and China. Together, they make the government look reactive rather than commanding.

But the government has counter-narratives:

“We are leading on AI safety.”
“We are cracking down on visa overstayers.”
“We are attracting world-class tech investment.”
“We are protecting Australians online.”
So the next week will be a fight over framing:

Is Australia a bold AI regulator?
Or is it another country getting ambushed by big tech and bad logistics?
The business mood
Business sentiment will be split:

AI/data-centre optimism stays strong because of Firmus, compute demand, and the “Australia has land and energy” narrative.
Rate anxiety stays high because of RBA warnings, mortgage pressure, and weak auction clearance.
Private credit and regulation are a smaller but growing worry, especially with the corporate regulator calling out poor practices and the Greensill settlement still not fully closed.
The market will be watching:

RBA signals,
Firmus IPO appetite,
ASX tech strength,
and any new details on the F-35 story.
The public mood
Ordinary Australians will probably feel three things most strongly:

AI is no longer abstract — it can now touch government systems.
Rates are still a household stressor — even if the economy looks okay on paper, mortgages and housing feel real.
Borders are getting stricter — especially for visitors and working holidaymakers.

Australia next week will be a country trying to look like an AI leader while getting ambushed by an AI agent, a defence logistics blunder, rising rate fear, and a border crackdown that is politically useful but socially noisy.
