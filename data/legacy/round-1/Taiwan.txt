Here is my uncensored read of the headlines and what I think will happen in Taiwan over the next week, roughly Sept 22–29.

Overall forecast
Taiwan will spend the next week under a diplomatic squeeze: not at war, but being used as pressure by both Washington and Beijing during the Trump–Xi summit cycle. The most likely outcome is managed tension, not a big rupture. Taipei will try to show strength through military drills, tech momentum, and quiet diplomacy, while Beijing will increase low-intensity pressure around the island and try to shape Taiwan’s domestic politics.

My best single prediction: no major military shock in Taiwan next week, but more visible cross-strait signaling, continued delay on some US arms sales, strong but volatile chip-market news, and a diplomatic push by Taipei to keep its international space open.

Most likely things to happen in Taiwan next week
1. The Trump–Xi summit will dominate the story, even though it happens in the US
This is the biggest factor. Xi is expected to press Trump on arms sales to Taiwan, trade, Iran, and AI. Taipei will be watching every word of any joint statement.

What I expect:

Xi will ask the US to slow, stagger, or limit new arms sales to Taiwan.
Trump will probably avoid saying he is “selling Taiwan” but may hint that the issue can be managed.
The US will likely reassure Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan after the summit.
Taiwan’s government will downplay any major policy shift, even if allies are nervous.
Likelihood: high, around 70–80%.

2. US arms sales to Taiwan will probably stay delayed, not fully approved or fully blocked
The Anduril story is important. If a major defense firm is publicly complaining that Trump’s delay is affecting business, that means the arms-sale issue is real and politically sensitive.

What I expect:

No huge surprise arms package inside the next week.
Possibly some smaller approvals, announcements, or negotiations continuing in the background.
The US may use Taiwan arms sales as a bargaining chip with China, especially around trade, semiconductors, or rare earths.
Allies and US defense firms will push for clarity.
Likelihood of continued delay: high, around 75%.
Likelihood of a major new package announced within the week: low to medium, maybe 20–30%.

3. China will likely increase pressure around Taiwan, but probably not full-scale war games
The headlines about Chinese Coast Guard activity east of Taiwan are significant. That is a quiet way for Beijing to expand its footprint without immediately triggering a crisis.

What I expect:

More Chinese naval or coast guard activity near or east of Taiwan.
Possible drone overflights or patrol incursions, but not necessarily a full blockade-style exercise.
China may use the period around the summit and before Taiwan’s Oct 10 national day to test reactions.
Taipei will respond with visible readiness, but probably avoid major escalation.
Likelihood of increased Chinese activity: high, around 75%.
Likelihood of a major full-scale military crisis: low, maybe 10–20%.

4. Taiwan will keep showing military strength without overdoing it
The live-fire drills with missiles and attack drones are a clear signal. Taipei wants to show that it is preparing for war while also telling the US and allies that it is not panicking.

What I expect:

More media focus on drone capability, missile defense, and readiness.
Possibly additional exercises, training announcements, or military demonstrations.
The government will frame this as “practical deterrence,” not provocation.
Domestic debate may intensify over whether Taiwan is preparing for war or being dragged into a US–China bargaining process.
Likelihood: high, around 70%.

5. Beijing will try to court Taiwan’s opposition and domestic politics
The Wall Street Journal headline about Xi seeing a “rare window” to convince Taiwan that resistance is futile is very important. It suggests China is not only applying external pressure but also trying to split or soften Taiwan politically.

What I expect:

More low-key cross-strait contact, possibly involving opposition figures, business groups, academics, religious groups, or local politicians.
Beijing may try to stage something symbolic: a visit, a meeting, a cultural event, or a “landmark” trip that it can claim as proof that Taiwan’s political space is opening.
Taipei will likely frame any contact as pragmatic and technical, not strategic.
Domestic politics in Taiwan may get more heated over whether cross-strait contact is “softening” the island or being realistic.
Likelihood of some kind of cross-strait political signal: medium to high, around 55–65%.
Likelihood of a major surprise visit or announcement: lower, maybe 25–35%.

6. Taiwan’s tech sector will stay in the spotlight, especially TSMC
The TSMC headlines are very strong:

3-nanometer mass production ceremony in Tainan.
Advanced packaging park groundbreaking in Kaohsiung.
Taiex hitting record highs on AI optimism.
Global chip stocks volatile after AI leaders talk about slowing development.
What I expect:

More news around TSMC, advanced packaging, AI supply chains, and semiconductor capacity.
The Taiex will probably stay near high levels but can swing sharply if AI sentiment changes.
If US–China talks produce some kind of AI or chip framework, that will move Taiwan’s market.
The “chip stocks tumble after AI leaders call for slowdown” headline is a warning: the rally is strong, but it can reverse quickly.
Likelihood of continued tech-sector focus: very high, around 85%.
Likelihood of major positive TSMC-related news: medium to high, maybe 60%.
Likelihood of sharp market volatility: high, around 70%.

7. The central bank will probably stay hawkish and patient
Taiwan held rates for the tenth consecutive quarter while raising growth and inflation forecasts. That suggests the economy is strong enough that the central bank does not feel forced to cut rates immediately, even if headline inflation slowed.

What I expect:

No sudden rate change in the next week.
More domestic discussion about growth, wages, AI investment, and whether the economy is overheating.
The government may use the strong economic data to argue that Taiwan is resilient despite geopolitical pressure.
Likelihood of a rate cut next week: low, under 10%.
Likelihood of continued “strong economy, cautious policy” messaging: high, around 80%.

8. Micron’s labor dispute may become more visible
The Micron employee protest in Taoyuan is an important domestic story. It shows that Taiwan’s tech boom is creating wage pressure and labor expectations.

What I expect:

More meetings between employees and management.
Possible protests, press conferences, or union statements.
The government may stay quiet publicly but watch the case closely because it affects foreign chipmakers and worker sentiment.
A deal is possible, but not guaranteed within the week.
Likelihood of continued dispute: high, around 70%.
Likelihood of a formal settlement inside the next week: medium, maybe 35–45%.

9. Taiwan will push quiet diplomacy to keep its international space open
Several headlines point in the same direction:

Taiwan Coast Guard planning first visit to Philippines in decades.
Taiwan launching a new global entity.
Philippines pushing back on China over Taiwan’s planned office in Cebu.
New Zealand ships transiting the Taiwan Strait.
Asian Games politics involving Taiwan’s representation.
What I expect:

More diplomatic or quasi-diplomatic moves by Taipei.
Possible announcements about the Philippines, APEC follow-up, or the new international organization.
China will likely protest or object to some of these moves.
Taiwan will try to normalize its presence without making it look like a direct challenge to Beijing.
Likelihood of additional diplomatic activity: high, around 70%.
Likelihood of a major surprise diplomatic breakthrough: low to medium, maybe 20–30%.

10. The Asia Games and international representation will remain a sore point
The Asian Games headlines show that even sports are becoming political. The welcoming ceremony involving Taiwan is already creating friction.

What I expect:

More complaints or diplomatic maneuvering over Taiwan’s name, flag, anthem, or ceremonial role.
Beijing may try to limit Taiwan’s visibility.
Taipei will push for recognition and dignity in international events.
This will feed into the broader “China squeezing Taiwan’s space” narrative.
Likelihood: medium to high, around 60%.

What I think is less likely but still possible
A surprise US arms package
Possible, but not very likely inside the next week. Trump may want to keep it as leverage during or after the Xi summit. If he wants a bigger deal with China, he may hold back major sales.

A major Chinese naval exercise east of Taiwan
Possible. The Coast Guard deployment east of the island is a quiet expansion. If the US responds strongly after the summit, China could escalate the visibility of its patrols.

A cross-strait political visit or symbolic meeting
Possible, especially if Beijing wants to show that it can reach into Taiwan’s domestic politics. It may not be a top-level leader visit, but it could involve opposition figures, local politicians, business leaders, or civil society groups.

A bigger Micron labor action
Possible if management does not move quickly on wages, benefits, or working conditions. The protest is already large enough to matter because Micron is a major foreign employer and semiconductor company in Taiwan.

A TSMC production milestone announcement
Very possible. The 3-nanometer mass production story and advanced packaging park are exactly the kind of things that can generate more headlines within days.

A surprise defense or trade deal involving Taiwan
Lower probability, but not zero. If Trump wants to show Xi he is getting something, there could be a package combining trade, chips, and arms sales. But if it happens, it will probably be framed as “strategic stability,” not as a Taiwan deal.

What would change the forecast
Watch for these signals:

The language in any Trump–Xi joint statement
If the US says Taiwan is an “internal Chinese affair,” that is bad for Taipei.
If the US says it wants to manage arms sales, that is a soft concession.
If the US strongly reaffirms support without mentioning concessions, that is better for Taiwan.
US arms sale approvals
A major package announced quickly after the summit would mean Trump is not fully buying Xi’s demand.
Continued silence would mean the delay is real.
Chinese naval activity east of Taiwan
If Chinese ships keep appearing there, it means Beijing is trying to build a new normal.
If there are large-scale drills or missile launches, the risk level rises.
TSMC and AI chip news
If TSMC announces major production progress, that supports Taiwan’s economic confidence.
If global AI leaders push for a slowdown, chip stocks may fall hard, including in Taiwan.
Cross-strait political contact
A visible visit or meeting would show Beijing is trying to split Taiwan politically.
No contact at all would mean the summit is not yet producing cross-strait movement.
Micron negotiations
If employees get significant concessions, it becomes a labor win.
If management digs in, protests may continue.
My bottom-line prediction for Taiwan next week
Taiwan will not be “given away” at the Trump–Xi summit, but it will be treated as a bargaining variable. The next week will probably look like this:

Diplomatically: Taipei will push quiet international outreach while Washington and Beijing talk about managing the Taiwan issue.
Militarily: China will increase pressure around the island; Taiwan will show readiness but avoid major escalation.
Economically: TSMC, AI chips, and record market levels will dominate business news, with high volatility possible.
Politically: Beijing will try to court Taiwan’s opposition or create a symbolic cross-strait moment.
Domestically: The Micron labor dispute and tech-sector wage pressure may become more visible.
In short: no war next week, no formal independence move, no full blockade — but more pressure, more signaling, more economic strength, and more diplomatic maneuvering.
