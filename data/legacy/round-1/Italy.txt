Here is my straight, un-PC one-week forecast for Italy, roughly Monday 28 September to Sunday 4 October 2026. I am treating the headlines as a signal map, not as gospel. No tables, just copy-paste friendly text.

Bottom line first
Italy does not look like it is about to have a full government collapse in the next seven days. Meloni’s government is still stable, and it has now become the longest-serving postwar Italian government. But that stability is under pressure from three directions at once:

The far-right flank is getting restless, especially Roberto Vannacci’s breakaway party and internal rivals inside Salvini’s League.
The EU budget story is becoming awkward after ISTAT confirmed a 3.1% deficit for 2025, while Meloni still wants to claim Italy will get back under the 3% ceiling in 2026.
The new school decree on veils and foreign students is a culture-war flashpoint that will keep generating protests, legal arguments, media fights, and coalition tension.
So my main prediction is: Meloni’s government survives the week, but she spends it defending her flank, managing the budget story, and trying to keep the market calm while Salvini and Vannacci try to steal the right-wing narrative.

The economy is not in panic mode yet, but bonds will test her credibility. The football team will be under pressure after the Belgium loss. Bank consolidation rumors will get louder. Fuel prices may get some short-term relief. The Colosseum should reopen with a safety story attached to it. And the nuclear power law becomes less about “will we do it?” and more about “when, where, and how fast?”

1. Politics: Meloni gets squeezed from the right and by Brussels
Most likely political story: school decree fallout
The decree limiting classrooms to 30% students with insufficient Italian-language skills and banning full face coverings in schools is now the biggest domestic culture-war issue.

I expect this to dominate the next week in several ways:

The decree will be published or formally pushed through the official state process, triggering immediate legal and political scrutiny.
Opposition parties will attack it as either “too little” or “too much,” depending on their base.
Parents, teachers’ unions, local school administrators, and integration groups will start organizing small protests or public statements, especially in big cities like Milan, Rome, Turin, Bologna, and Naples.
Regional governments may begin asking questions about implementation: how schools count “insufficient Italian,” how they handle mixed classrooms, what happens if a class exceeds the 30% cap, and whether the veil ban applies to all levels of schooling.
The far-right will use it as a campaign issue ahead of 2027, even if the decree is technically only about schools.
My prediction: this does not become a national crisis immediately, but it becomes a recurring headline for the rest of the year. Next week you will see protests, legal challenges, social media fights, and coalition partners trying to decide whether to support Meloni loudly or quietly.

Budget and deficit: the real pressure point
The ISTAT number is important. A 3.1% deficit for 2025 is not a disaster by itself, but it is politically inconvenient because Meloni wants to say Italy is back on the right fiscal path. The government now has to explain how it will get the 2026 deficit below 3% without cutting too much spending or angering its coalition partners.

I expect the next week to include:

More meetings between the Ministry of Economy, coalition parties, and possibly EU officials.
Meloni publicly defending the 2026 target while trying not to promise painful cuts.
Salvini’s League pushing for spending credibility, tax relief, or at least no major new burdens on households and businesses.
Fratelli d’Italia trying to keep the government looking fiscally responsible enough for Brussels and the bond market.
Opposition parties attacking Meloni for either “reckless spending” or “hiding behind technicalities.”
Bond markets watching Italy closely, especially because France’s budget battle is already stressing European sovereign debt.
My prediction: Italy’s 10-year BTP yield may wobble upward during the week if the market senses that Meloni is under pressure to spend rather than consolidate. It does not need to become a full sell-off, but the spread versus Germany or France could widen a little. The key question will be whether Rome can make its 2026 deficit plan sound credible before the autumn stability program process gets fully underway.

In plain terms: Meloni wants to be seen as the strongwoman who fixed Italy’s finances and integration policy at the same time. That is possible, but it is going to be hard.

Salvini and the League: internal power struggle
Salvini calling an extraordinary League congress is a warning sign. It suggests he feels his grip on the party is being tested by internal rivals and declining poll support.

Next week I expect:

Factional maneuvering inside the League.
Rivals pushing for more aggressive positions on immigration, taxes, pensions, or regional autonomy.
Salvini trying to present himself as the unifier while also looking strong enough to punish dissenters.
Media speculation about whether the congress will produce a major policy shift or just a leadership photo-op.
My prediction: no formal collapse of the League next week, but more open tension. If Salvini looks too weak, Meloni’s coalition becomes more fragile. If he looks dominant, he may push harder to make sure the League is seen as the real engine of the right-wing project, not just a junior partner to Fratelli d’Italia.

Vannacci and the far-right split vote
Roberto Vannacci’s party is now becoming a real wildcard. The fact that it is getting its first serious parliamentary test is important because it gives the anti-Meloni far-right a visible vehicle.

I expect one of two outcomes:

If Vannacci’s party wins or comes close in the local/parliamentary contest, Meloni will feel immediate pressure from her right flank. The story becomes: “Why did she need this decree? Was it enough? Is Vannacci now the real face of the hard-right?”
If Vannacci loses but still gets a strong vote share, the damage is smaller in the short term, but the message remains: Meloni’s right is not fully satisfied.
My prediction: Vannacci does not immediately unseat Meloni, but he becomes a constant irritant. The next week will show whether his movement is a local curiosity or a national force. If it wins, expect more coalition tension. If it loses, expect Vannacci to frame the result as “first step” and keep pressing.

2. Economy and business: bank rumors, fuel prices, and defensive deals
Bank consolidation chatter gets louder
The banking headlines are important because Italian banks are a major part of the country’s financial identity and political economy. The names involved — UniCredit, Credit Agricole, Banco BPM, Intesa Sanpaolo, Monte dei Paschi di Siena — guarantee media attention.

I expect next week to include:

More rumors or semi-official statements about possible joint moves around Banco BPM.
Market speculation that Italian bank consolidation is entering a new phase.
Possible antitrust concerns if the deals get too big.
Shareholder and board reactions, especially if any party wants to protect independence or maximize value.
Government officials trying to sound neutral while privately caring about stability, employment, and regional banking power.
My prediction: no completed mega-merger in the next seven days, but the story will heat up. There may be an official statement, a denial, a confirmation of “exploratory talks,” or a market-moving hint. Bank stocks could become more volatile. The bigger issue is whether Italian banking consolidation becomes a political project again after years of partial integration.

Intesa and the AI fraud story
The report that Fideuram, the private banking arm of Intesa Sanpaolo, lost around €95 million to an AI impersonation scam is a very modern headline for Italy. It mixes old-money banking with new-tech fraud.

I expect:

Intesa to issue a more detailed statement.
An internal investigation or board-level review.
Media questions about cybersecurity, executive approval processes, and whether other Italian banks have similar exposure.
A broader debate about AI fraud in European finance.
My prediction: this becomes a short but intense corporate governance story. It is not likely to shake Intesa’s fundamentals next week, but it will make the bank look more exposed than it would like. If any senior executive is named or if the amount gets revised upward, expect more headlines.

Fuel prices and energy security
Eni cutting pump prices for at least a month and the government convening oil refiners to boost fuel output shows that Meloni is trying to shield consumers before winter. This is politically useful because fuel prices are one of the few economic issues ordinary Italians feel directly.

I expect:

More refiners or distributors following Eni with price adjustments.
Government announcements about supporting domestic fuel output or stabilizing pump prices.
Meloni using the story as proof that her government is protecting households.
Opposition attacks if prices do not fall enough or if the measures look temporary.
A background worry about Red Sea shipping, Houthi attacks, and energy imports from the Middle East.
My prediction: short-term fuel price relief becomes a political talking point. It may not solve all cost-of-living problems, but it gives Meloni an easy win to communicate before 2027. If shipping disruptions worsen, the story flips from “consumer relief” to “energy security.”

Defense and infrastructure deals
The Leonardo/Fincantieri destroyer contract and the Eaton acquisition of COL Group are not mass-market headlines, but they matter for Italy’s industrial policy.

I expect:

Local pride in shipbuilding and defense industry regions.
Parliamentary questions about jobs, subcontracting, and long-term capacity.
Union reactions to the Eaton/COL deal, especially around layoffs, relocations, or integration into a foreign group.
More discussion about Italy’s need to become a stronger European defense and grid-equipment player.
My prediction: these deals generate steady business headlines rather than political shocks. They support the narrative that Italy is modernizing its industrial base, but they may also create local friction if workers or smaller suppliers feel squeezed.

3. Nuclear power: from symbolic approval to implementation anxiety
The Senate’s final approval of nuclear power is a major policy shift after decades without new reactors. But next week will not be about building a reactor. It will be about the political and technical aftermath.

I expect:

Government officials talking about small modular reactors, timelines, site selection, grid upgrades, and investment plans.
Environmental groups organizing protests or statements, especially in regions associated with old nuclear sites or energy infrastructure.
Opposition parties using the issue to attack Meloni on cost, safety, waste disposal, and dependency on foreign technology.
Energy companies and banks watching for tender signals.
A public debate about whether Italy is really ready or just opening the door symbolically.
My prediction: the nuclear story moves from “approved” to “when?” Next week will be full of promises, timelines, and careful wording. If the government gives a concrete schedule, expect a media surge. If it stays vague, critics will say Meloni is using nuclear as a photo opportunity.

4. Football: Mancini’s job becomes more fragile
Italy’s 0-2 loss to Belgium in Mancini’s second debut was not just a bad result. It happened in front of a half-empty Stadio Olimpico, with boos and a rebuilding narrative already under pressure after missing the World Cup. That makes the next fixture very important.

I expect:

Mancini to make tactical changes or give more minutes to younger players.
Media debate about whether he is the right man for the rebuild.
Fans to be impatient, especially if the team looks passive again.
The next Nations League match to become a pressure point rather than a routine game.
If Italy wins, the narrative softens: “new coach needs time.”
If Italy draws poorly or loses again, the boos get louder and calls for a change begin to sound more serious.
My prediction: Mancini can survive one bad start, but not two. The next match is effectively a make-or-break moment for his short-term credibility. A win does not fix everything, but it gives him breathing space. Another loss makes the rebuild look fragile and forces the media to ask whether Italy has a plan or just another interim-style project.

The tennis and cycling results are positive sports stories, but football is the emotional center of national mood in Italy. If the Azzurri play badly again, it will color the whole week.

5. Diplomacy and security: US ties, Red Sea risks, and Tajani’s image problem
US critical minerals deal
The memorandum between Marco Rubio and Antonio Tajani on critical minerals is strategically important. It shows Italy trying to position itself as a reliable European partner for the US in batteries, defense, rare materials, and supply chains.

I expect:

Domestic debate about how deep Italy’s alignment with Washington goes.
Opposition criticism that the government is making strategic decisions without enough parliamentary scrutiny.
Industry interest from mining, energy, defense, and manufacturing firms.
Government messaging that this is about sovereignty, not dependence.
My prediction: this becomes a quiet but important foreign-policy story. It will not dominate daily headlines like the school decree, but it matters for Italy’s long-term positioning between Europe, the US, and global supply chains.

Red Sea and Houthi risk
Italy is exposed to shipping and energy risks in the Mediterranean and Red Sea corridor. The G7 focus on Houthis and Iran means Rome will keep talking about naval deployments, shipping protection, and energy security.

I expect:

Italian officials making statements about protecting merchant shipping.
Possible naval or diplomatic coordination with EU and US partners.
If an incident involving European or Italian-linked shipping occurs, the story escalates quickly.
My prediction: no major new war-level shock next week unless something happens at sea, but Italy will keep emphasizing preparedness. The government wants to look strong on security without being dragged into another open-ended mission.

Tajani and the AI skirt meme
The AI-generated images of Foreign Minister Antonio Tajani wearing skirts after his alleged sexist remarks are a very 2026 political image problem. It is funny, annoying, and politically useful for opponents.

I expect:

The meme to keep circulating.
Journalists to ask him about it at the next public appearance.
He may either make a joke, give a stiff answer, or try to move on quickly.
Coalition partners hoping the story fades without embarrassing the government too much.
My prediction: this is more of an image irritant than a policy crisis. But if Tajani handles it badly, it becomes another example of the government being caught in culture-war moments at the same time as it passes the school decree.

6. Cities, culture, and local crises
Colosseum reopens with a safety story
The closure after a worker fell to his death is significant because the Colosseum is Italy’s most visited monument. It also touches on workplace safety, tourism management, and Rome’s ability to maintain its iconic sites.

I expect:

A safety review before reopening.
Possible temporary capacity limits or restricted areas.
Unions demanding checks at other heritage sites.
Media focus on the lighting installation work and contractor arrangements.
The Colosseum reopening within days, but with a darker narrative attached.
My prediction: the Colosseum reopens, probably within the week, but not without new safety rules or public discussion. If the review finds major issues, there could be partial restrictions for longer. A second incident would become a national scandal, but that is less likely next week.

Naples cash raid and hidden economy
Finding €3.2 million hidden inside walls, floors, and furniture in Naples is a classic Italian anti-mafia/anti-money-laundering story with modern scale.

I expect:

More arrests or court filings.
Asset seizures.
Prosecutors explaining the drug/extortion ring behind the raid.
Media stories about cash hidden in ordinary apartments.
Political use of the story by anti-mafia groups and local opposition.
My prediction: this becomes a rolling crime story over the next several days. It may not change national politics, but it reinforces the image of Italy fighting its old shadow economy while trying to modernize.

Milan airport named after Berlusconi
Naming Milan’s second-busiest airport after Silvio Berlusconi is symbolically important. It cements his place in Italian public life, but it also gives opponents another cultural-political target.

I expect:

Some ceremonial or official acknowledgment.
Opposition criticism about appropriateness, legacy, and political symbolism.
Supporters framing it as a deserved honor.
The issue becoming more prominent if there is a plaque ceremony or public event.
My prediction: minor but persistent controversy. It will not dominate the week, but it adds to the Berlusconi legacy debate.

Lake Como missing diver
The British man who went missing after diving into Lake Como to rescue a teenager is a human-interest story that can become international if the search drags on.

I expect:

Continued coastguard and police searches.
Updates from local authorities.
If he is found alive, a relief story with questions about conditions.
If he is not found quickly, growing media attention and public sympathy.
My prediction: this remains a local tragedy with international interest. It may produce one or two major updates during the week, but it is unlikely to shape national politics unless something dramatic happens.

7. Agriculture and food: wine harvest becomes a logistics problem
The Piedmont wine headlines are interesting because they mix good news with a practical problem: the vintage is excellent, but cellars are full. That means quality selection, storage pressure, and possible price implications for growers.

I expect:

Wine boards and regional authorities discussing storage solutions.
Growers choosing between keeping top grapes and selling lower-quality stock.
Export discussions as Italy tries to push premium wine abroad.
Media focus on the “beautiful vintage” versus full cellars dilemma.
My prediction: this is a positive but complicated story. It shows Italian agriculture can produce excellence, but it also reveals infrastructure limits. If prices for certain grapes or wines move noticeably, the story becomes more economic.
