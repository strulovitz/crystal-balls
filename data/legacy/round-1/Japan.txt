1) Typhoon Dujuan dominates the first half of the week
This is the most immediate story.

evacuation orders and warnings around Tokyo, the eastern coast, Kanto, Chubu, and nearby areas
flight cancellations and train delays
Shinkansen disruptions or temporary suspensions on affected lines

Because Japan is also coming out of a holiday period, the transport disruption will be amplified. Many people are traveling, many businesses are reopening, and logistics will be strained.

What I expect to happen next week from the typhoon
By Tuesday/Wednesday, the headlines will shift from “evacuation warnings” to:

damage reports
insurance claims
cleanup operations
delayed trains and flights resuming
businesses reporting supply chain or staffing problems
local governments asking for disaster support funds
media focus on whether evacuation orders were followed properly
possible secondary weather disturbance later in the week, which could keep JMA warnings alive
This is not just a minor rain story. The rare evacuation alert near Tokyo makes this a major domestic governance moment. If there are deaths, landslides, or major infrastructure damage, it will dominate political coverage for several days.

Why this matters politically
Takaichi just reshuffled the cabinet to revive sagging support. A typhoon is a stress test.

If disaster response looks competent:

approval ratings may get a small boost
the government can claim stability and preparedness
If things look chaotic:

opposition parties will attack local and national officials
questions will rise about aging infrastructure, flood defenses, evacuation planning, and emergency communication
So I expect the typhoon to become both a weather story and a political story.

4) Markets, the BOJ, and the yen will stay volatile
This is one of the most important storylines.

The BOJ hiked to 1.25%, a 31-year high, but the market reaction was not the clean “strong yen” moment many expected. Instead:

the yen weakened
JGB yields moved in a complicated way
the Nikkei still rose
traders remain focused on policy credibility, fiscal risk, and global rates
I expect the next week to be marked by:

continued yen weakness or sharp two-way volatility
Ministry of Finance rate checks and verbal warnings
speculation about possible intervention if USD/JPY moves further
JGB yield fluctuations as markets weigh BOJ tightening against fiscal expansion
Japanese corporate earnings season continuing, with weak yen affecting importers and exporters differently
institutional investors rebalancing after the BOJ decision
Yen: what I expect
The yen is vulnerable. The headlines already say so explicitly.

I expect one of these three paths next week:

Most likely:
The yen stays weak but unstable, with occasional sharp spikes and MOF warning language.
Possible:
If USD/JPY pushes higher, Japan conducts a verbal intervention or limited FX operation. That would create a short-term spike, then fade unless policy expectations shift.
Less likely but important:
A sharper yen move if global risk appetite changes suddenly or if BOJ officials sound more hawkish than expected.
Why the yen is so sensitive right now
Several forces are colliding:

BOJ tightening is not yet strong enough to fully reprice currency risk
fiscal policy looks expansionary
oil import costs are rising
trade deficits are widening
US rates remain important for global capital flows
Japanese investors may start rebalancing after domestic rates rise
That means the yen will remain a headline magnet.

BOJ tone next week
There is no new policy meeting immediately, but I expect:

more commentary from BOJ officials
debates about whether inflation risks are still an “overshoot” risk
discussion of whether subsidies are masking underlying price pressure
market focus on whether the next hike could come sooner than expected if oil and imported inflation stay high
The key question for markets is not just “did the BOJ hike?”
It is: does fiscal policy undermine monetary credibility?

That tension will define Japanese financial news next week.
