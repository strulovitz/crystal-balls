Straight answer: over the next seven days, France will look like a country trying to hold four fires at once — the Pope’s visit, the budget/debt crisis, the police “licence to kill” bill, and the Red Sea / Iran / energy pressure from abroad. I would not expect chaos, but I do expect visible stress, more protests, nervous bond markets, and a sharper French diplomatic posture in the Middle East.

My forecast for roughly September 26 to October 3:

1. The Pope’s visit will end as a major soft-power success, but it will leave political sparks behind

High confidence this is one of the main stories of the week.

The final days of Pope Leo XIV’s trip — especially anything connected to Lourdes and Metz — will draw large crowds, heavy security, and intense media coverage.
The Church will claim a victory: huge youth turnout, renewed Catholic visibility, baptisms rising, and a pope who can talk about AI, Europe, and moral questions in a very French way.
But the secular side of French politics will not disappear. Expect continued debate over laïcité, state resources for a religious visit, the abuse-survivors meeting, assisted dying, and whether the Church is getting too much public space.
There may be small counter-protests, especially around Paris or Lourdes, but I would not expect them to dominate unless there is a security incident or a scandal involving the Vatican.
The “paradise of machines” AI warning will probably become a national talking point for several days after the visit. French tech leaders, ministers, and cultural figures will use it to argue about whether France/Europe should regulate AI more firmly or keep the sector competitive.
Bottom line: the papal visit is likely to end on a high note emotionally, but politically it will feed existing culture wars rather than settle them.

2. The budget and debt problem becomes the biggest domestic pressure point

High confidence this dominates the second half of the week.

France’s debt is already being framed as a record problem: near 120% of GDP in 2026, possibly worse in 2027.
The Lecornu government will likely use the next few days to defend or sharpen its plan for cuts and adjustments worth around 54 billion euros.
The 10-year OAT yield is already under pressure above 4.5%. If the budget details are seen as too weak, or if coalition partners fight over who pays, bond yields could move higher again.
I would expect the opposition to attack hard:
the far left will push its “debt fire” / cancellation-of-ECB-bonds idea;
the right and centrist critics will say the government is too slow or too indecisive;
market commentators will keep asking whether France is becoming the next big eurozone fiscal risk after Italy.
The government probably survives this week, but it will look more fragile than usual. If there is a bad bond auction, a visible coalition split, or a failed vote on a key text, no-confidence talk becomes much louder.
Most likely outcome: no immediate collapse, but the budget fight becomes the main domestic story after the Pope leaves.

3. Protests against the police “licence to kill” bill continue, and may become more organized

High confidence there is another round of street activity.

The first marches have already happened, so the next week will likely bring either:
a second national wave of protests, probably around Sunday or early next week;
or more targeted demonstrations outside ministries, prefectures, or the National Assembly.
Police unions and some left groups will keep pushing the “licence to kill” framing because it is simple and emotionally powerful.
The government has two likely options:
make cosmetic changes to the bill to reduce the symbolism;
or push through with parliamentary procedure, which would anger the opposition and increase the risk of no-confidence motions.
I would not expect the bill to be completely withdrawn in seven days. It is too politically linked to police morale and security politics for that.
Most likely: continued street pressure, more televised confrontations between protesters and police, and a parliamentary fight if the text moves forward.

4. France begins moving toward real military involvement around Saudi Arabia / the Red Sea

High confidence this becomes concrete in the next few days.

Macron has already said France will send troops and defence systems to help protect Yanbu and the Red Sea oil route.
In the next week, expect:
more details on what exactly is being sent — ships, aircraft, radar, air-defence systems, command elements;
diplomatic coordination with the United States, Saudi Arabia, and possibly Gulf partners;
French officials arguing this is protection of a vital energy route, not a full war.
This will be framed at home as France acting like a major power again: present in the Middle East, protecting European energy interests, and not just waiting for Washington.
The risk is that if Houthi attacks continue or there is a serious strike on Saudi oil infrastructure, France gets pulled deeper into a conflict it may not want.
Most likely: deployment preparations accelerate, but France stops short of saying it has entered the war directly.

5. The Hormuz / UN Security Council diplomacy becomes more visible

Medium-to-high confidence this stays in the news.

France is reportedly working with the United States on a draft UN Security Council resolution around freedom of navigation in the Strait of Hormuz.
In the next week, expect:
diplomatic consultations;
possible circulation of draft text;
Russian and Chinese resistance if the language gets too strong;
European debate over whether France is trying to create a new naval escort framework.
If Russia vetoes or blocks the resolution, France may fall back on a “coalition of the willing” approach — essentially doing more with allies without full Security Council blessing.
This fits Macron’s broader posture: France wants to be seen as an independent actor in global security, not just following the US.
Most likely: no dramatic breakthrough in seven days, but the diplomatic machinery starts moving.

6. Iran and France have a sharper diplomatic row, but probably not a full crisis yet

Medium confidence this escalates symbolically.

Iran has already closed a French language centre in Tehran.
France says it will summon Iran’s ambassador and take appropriate measures.
In the next week, expect:
the formal summoning of Iran’s ambassador in Paris;
Iranian protests near the French embassy or consulate;
possible symbolic retaliation by Iran — freezing assets, closing another office, or making a diplomatic complaint;
French officials using the row to justify a tougher line on Iran.
A full rupture is less likely unless something bigger happens — for example, a new Houthi attack tied to Iran, an incident involving a French ship, or a stronger Iranian move against French interests.
Most likely: ugly diplomacy, more public friction, but not yet a major crisis.

7. The Usmanov sanctions issue keeps France in trouble with parts of the EU

Medium confidence this remains a background irritant.

Estonia and other states are already pushing back after France’s role in lifting or easing EU sanctions on Alisher Usmanov.
In the next week, expect:
more diplomatic complaints from Baltic and Eastern European states;
French officials defending the move as pragmatic or deal-based;
Russia using it as propaganda evidence that EU sanctions are weakening;
possible follow-up in EU foreign affairs discussions.
This is not likely to become a headline crisis by itself, but it feeds the broader narrative that France’s “realpolitik” style is straining European unity.
Most likely: continued grumbling inside the EU, not a full fight, but enough to keep French diplomats busy.

8. Energy, fuel prices, heat waves, and wildfires stay very much in the picture

Medium-to-high confidence this remains important.

Fuel prices are already at record highs, and France has ruled out across-the-board fuel tax cuts.
That creates a dangerous mix: high prices + no broad tax relief + heat/drought + nuclear output constraints + southern European wildfires.
In the next week, I would expect:
more public discontent among drivers, truckers, commuters, and rural voters;
possible small protests at gas stations or by transport unions;
the government resisting a big tax cut but possibly offering limited relief — for example targeted support, temporary measures, or grid/energy plans;
more coverage of heat-wave pressure on the power system, especially if drought continues to limit nuclear cooling water.
If the weather worsens, southern France could see another round of serious wildfires, evacuations, and debate over whether the state is ready for repeated climate shocks.
Most likely: energy and fuel remain a major source of public anger, but not yet a nationwide crisis unless prices keep climbing sharply or there is a visible power shortage.

9. The AI / literature scandal turns into a bigger culture war

Medium confidence this stays alive.

The Goncourt prize removing a novel after AI-use accusations is already a big story.
In the next week, expect:
the author to respond publicly or legally;
publishers, writers’ unions, and literary circles to argue about whether AI use should be banned, disclosed, or accepted;
the Pope’s warning about AI to make the issue feel less niche and more national.
This could become a symbolic fight about French culture: Is France defending human creativity, or is it becoming technophobic?
Most likely: no final legal resolution in seven days, but the controversy becomes a broader debate about AI in art, publishing, and education.

10. Football gives France a rare mood-lifter — but only if the results cooperate

High confidence this gets media attention; medium confidence it matters politically.

Zidane’s first training session has already happened, and Mbappé is still a huge story because of the Ballon d’Or narrative, his new On deal, and the post-World-Cup hangover.
If France plays another Nations League match in this window, expect:
heavy media focus on whether Zidane can bring back a sense of confidence;
Mbappé’s performance to dominate headlines;
the government and politicians to use any win as a small national morale boost.
If the result is poor, the criticism will be sharp because French football is still emotionally linked to national identity and post-World-Cup disappointment.
Most likely: football becomes a pressure valve for public mood — good if France wins, noisy if it does not.

11. Migrant crossings from northern France probably continue or increase

Medium confidence this stays visible.

The dinghy departures from places like Hardelot-Plage are already being reported.
In the next week, expect:
more small boat crossings;
at least one dramatic rescue or beaching if weather is favorable;
renewed debate in the north about Calais, Channel crossings, and border policy.
This is not likely to become the single biggest national story unless there is an incident with many people affected or a major political reaction.
Most likely: continued crossings, more local coverage, and another argument between left and right over migration management.

What I think is most likely to define the week
If I had to rank the main stories by importance:

Budget / debt / bond market pressure — the biggest structural risk.
Police bill protests — the biggest street-level story.
Pope’s final days and aftermath — the biggest prestige and culture story.
Red Sea / Saudi deployment and Hormuz diplomacy — the biggest foreign policy story.
Fuel prices, heat, wildfires, energy stress — the biggest daily-life irritant.
What I would watch for as warning signs
The forecast changes if any of these happen:

A major Houthi strike on Saudi oil or Red Sea shipping
Then France’s military role expands faster and markets react harder.
French bond yields jump sharply higher, especially toward 5% or above
That would turn a budget dispute into a real fiscal crisis story.
A visible split in the governing coalition over the 2027 budget
Then no-confidence talk becomes much more serious.
A security incident during the Pope’s visit
Even a small one could shift the narrative from “historic success” to “security and laïcité debate.”
A big protest day that goes beyond the expected police bill marches
If fuel prices, energy costs, and police protections merge into one broader movement, the government looks more exposed.
My bottom-line prediction
France will probably end this week looking like a country with global ambitions but domestic fragility.

The Pope’s visit is likely to be remembered as a major success for France’s international image.
But the budget fight, bond market nerves, and police bill protests will make the government look more vulnerable than it wants.
Abroad, France will push harder in the Red Sea / Hormuz file and accept more diplomatic friction with Iran and parts of the EU.
At home, fuel prices, heat, wildfires, and energy stress will keep ordinary people irritated even if the big political stories dominate.
In short: no full crisis in seven days, but a clear step toward a more tense, more exposed French government.
