Okay — no tables, no fluff. I read the headlines as a pattern, not as separate stories. For the next week in the Democratic Republic of Congo, my prediction is that the country will be juggling four big things at once:

The Ebola crisis keeps spreading and dominating public fear.
The constitutional fight over President Tshisekedi’s possible third term turns into more street protests and political pressure.
The eastern front stays tense, with M23/Rwanda issues becoming even more central because Bukavu appears to be under rebel control or heavy rebel influence.
The US minerals push accelerates, especially around copper, cobalt, critical minerals, the Lobito corridor, and the new DRC-US task force.
My blunt forecast: next week will not look like one single dramatic event. It will look like a pile-up of bad health news, political anger in Kinshasa, rebel-held eastern cities, and economic announcements about minerals and exports.

Here is what I expect to happen.

First: Ebola will remain the biggest national anxiety

I expect more confirmed Ebola cases and deaths over the next week, even if some officials start saying the outbreak may be peaking. The headlines are already mixed: government sources say infection rates are slowing, while the UN and others say it is still growing or too early to call a peak. That contradiction will continue.

The case in the seventh province, South-Ubangi, is important because it shows the virus is no longer just an eastern problem. It has moved into the northwest, near borders with the Central African Republic and Congo-Brazzaville. That will increase fear of long-distance spread.

I would expect:

More contact tracing around the South-Ubangi case.
More statements from Uganda, Rwanda, CAR, and Congo-Brazzaville about border controls or health checks.
Possible tighter restrictions at some land crossings, especially if travel history shows movement through multiple countries.
More public fear around funerals, schools, markets, and hospitals.
More official confusion between “cases are declining” and “the outbreak is still massive.”
The government will probably keep saying the worst may be over or near. The UN, WHO, Africa CDC, and field health workers will probably say it is too early to celebrate. I expect that tension to continue through the week.

I would not be surprised if by the end of next week there are either:

More cases in existing affected provinces,
Or a new district/province mentioned, especially if the virus has already moved through transport routes.
The outbreak is large enough now that even a “downward trend” will still produce scary headlines because the absolute numbers remain high.

Second: Bukavu school fire will become a political and emotional flashpoint

The fire in Bukavu, with reports of around 24 to 29 children killed, is not just a tragedy. It is happening in a city described as rebel-held or under M23 influence. That makes it politically explosive.

I expect the official death toll to settle somewhere around 27 to 29, possibly higher if delayed reports come in. The number will matter less than the political blame game.

Next week I would expect:

More funerals and memorial services.
Public anger over school safety, overcrowding, fire exits, building codes, and emergency response.
M23 or local rebel-aligned authorities trying to show they can govern by announcing compensation, reconstruction, security measures, or an inquiry.
Kinshasa blaming M23, Rwanda, and poor local administration.
Opposition politicians using the tragedy to attack both the government and the rebels.
Possible calls for a national commission of inquiry.
Because Bukavu is such a big city, if M23 really controls it or acts as the effective power there, that will be used as proof that the eastern crisis is no longer just a border war. It is now a governance crisis. The fire will make that point very emotionally.

I would not expect the fire itself to cause an immediate major battle, but it could increase pressure on M23 to either look more like an administration or face more local resentment.

Third: Constitutional protests over third term will keep escalating

The headlines about police tear gas, clashes between opposition and pro-government supporters in Kinshasa, and demonstrations against constitutional change suggest the political crisis is heating up.

This is probably one of the most important stories for the next week. If Tshisekedi is pushing a constitutional change that could allow a third term, the street fight will continue.

I expect:

More opposition rallies in Kinshasa and other cities.
More pro-government counter-rallies.
More police use of tear gas or crowd control.
Some arrests, especially if protests get close to government buildings, parliament, or party headquarters.
Possible roadblocks or brief curfews in sensitive areas if clashes intensify.
National Assembly activity around the constitutional amendment process.
Government attempts to frame the change as stability, continuity, or national consensus.
Opposition attempts to frame it as a power grab.
I do not think the constitution will be fully changed next week, but I would expect the government to push the process forward visibly. That could mean:

A special committee report,
A scheduled parliamentary vote,
An announcement of a referendum date,
Or a public statement saying the third-term question is “legally ready.”
The biggest risk is that opposition and pro-government crowds clash more violently. That could turn a political dispute into a street crisis. I would rate that as medium-to-high likelihood in the next week, especially if the government tries to force momentum after Tuesday’s clashes.

Fourth: M23/Rwanda front will stay tense, but no full breakthrough

The Geneva talks between Rwanda and DRC are important, but I would not expect a major peace deal in one week. These talks involve deep disputes over security guarantees, M23’s role, territorial control, borders, sovereignty, and regional influence.

I expect:

More technical or political sessions in Geneva.
Continued fighting or skirmishes in eastern DRC.
M23 consolidating its position in areas it controls.
DRC military claiming counteroffensives or defensive successes.
Rwanda denying direct control over M23 while still being accused of backing them.
Burundi being dragged into the blame game again.
UN, AU, and regional mediators pushing for a ceasefire or implementation plan.
The fact that Bukavu is described as rebel-held is huge. If true, M23 has moved beyond being a guerrilla force into something more like a territorial power in parts of the east. That will make the peace talks harder, not easier, because M23 now has more leverage.

Next week I would expect more diplomatic language than a real breakthrough. Possible headlines:

“Geneva talks continue but key issues remain unresolved.”
“DRC and Rwanda agree to further technical consultations.”
“M23 rejects or partially accepts mediation proposal.”
“Fighting continues around eastern front lines.”
A full ceasefire is possible, but I would rate it as medium likelihood, not high. A major military escalation is also possible if M23 tries to expand further or if DRC forces launch a serious counterattack, but I would not put that at the top of my predictions for just one week.

Fifth: US minerals push will become very visible

This is the economic story cutting through the crisis. The creation of a DRC-US task force, record copper exports, rising copper prices, Lobito corridor expansion, Rwanda tungsten deal, and US support for African critical minerals all point in the same direction: Washington wants to lock in DRC into a Western-aligned minerals supply chain.

I expect more announcements or meetings around:

Copper and cobalt export corridors.
Fast-track permits for mining projects.
Critical minerals strategy.
Lobito corridor capacity expansion.
Energy infrastructure along the corridor.
Geological data control by DRC.
Possible new deals with US or Western-backed companies.
The DRC government will likely use this in two ways:

To show that it is modernizing the economy and attracting serious foreign partners.
To raise revenue, especially from copper, which can then be used to fund Ebola response, infrastructure, political stabilization, and public projects.
I would expect at least one concrete announcement next week from the task force or related mining/export story. It may not be a giant signed treaty, but probably something like:

A roadmap for critical minerals exports.
A fast-track implementation plan.
A Lobito corridor financing or capacity update.
A statement about copper/cobalt export targets.
A meeting between DRC and US officials on mineral security.
This matters because the DRC is not just reacting to crisis. It is also trying to turn its mineral wealth into political and diplomatic leverage. The US wants minerals; DRC wants money, infrastructure, and strategic partnerships. That transaction will keep producing headlines.

What will probably not happen next week

I would not expect:

A full collapse of the state.
An immediate coup.
A complete end to the Ebola outbreak.
A final peace deal between DRC, Rwanda, and M23 in one week.
A single all-out national war declaration.
The constitutional amendment to be fully completed in seven days.
The country will feel unstable, but I would not predict total chaos in just one week. The bigger risk is that multiple crises feed each other: Ebola creates panic, protests create political pressure, M23’s territorial strength weakens the government’s image, and minerals money becomes the main tool for buying stability.

Bottom line

My best prediction is that by next weekend the DRC will be dealing with a triple crisis: epidemic, constitutional legitimacy, and eastern rebellion, while using its minerals wealth as an economic escape valve.

The most likely overall picture:

Ebola numbers still rising or plateauing at high levels.
More street protests in Kinshasa over the third-term push.
M23/Rwanda talks continuing but not resolving.
Bukavu fire becoming a symbol of eastern instability.
US minerals task force producing visible economic announcements.
Government claiming control while trying to use copper revenue and foreign partnerships to stabilize the situation.
In one sentence: next week in DRC will be dominated by Ebola fear, third-term street politics, rebel-held eastern cities, and a fast-moving US minerals deal machine.
