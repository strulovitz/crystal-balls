Here is my blunt forecast for the Netherlands over roughly the next 7 days, September 22–29, 2026. You asked for uncensored and not politically correct, so I’ll keep it direct: the Dutch government will look busy, slightly rattled, and forced to juggle international embarrassment, street politics, transport chaos, budget fights, and a new football coach all at once.

I’m ignoring the pure global noise and focusing on the items that will actually move Dutch public life.

The US sanctions against the ICC will dominate the week
Likelihood: high
This is the biggest external story touching the Netherlands directly because the International Criminal Court is based in The Hague. If Washington moves “soon,” I expect the formal announcement, or at least a very strong leak followed by official confirmation, to land inside this window.

What that means in practice:

The Dutch government will issue careful but firm statements defending the ICC while also sounding pragmatic about limiting damage.
Prime Minister Rob Jetten will probably try to position the Netherlands as calm, rule-of-law-minded, and not trying to pick a fight with Washington.
Dutch banks, payment processors, and IT vendors linked to the ICC will become a quiet focus. The government may issue guidance or ask institutions to prepare contingency plans.
The EU will be pulled into the story quickly. Expect emergency calls between Brussels, The Hague, Washington, and possibly other financial hubs such as London or Frankfurt.
Dutch opposition parties will use it politically. The left and liberal parties will say “defend the court,” while right-wing parties may ask why the Netherlands is hosting an institution that keeps irritating a major transatlantic partner.
The most likely outcome is not a full diplomatic rupture, but a week of tense diplomacy, media speculation about which ICC payments or staff members are affected, and Dutch officials trying to keep the court operational without making it look like The Hague is exposed.

If the US waits until after this week, I still expect heavy leaks, urgent Dutch-EU consultations, and public statements that the Netherlands is “monitoring closely” and “preparing for all scenarios.” Either way, the ICC story stays central.

More protests in The Hague and possibly Amsterdam
Likelihood: medium-high
The far-right protest in The Hague did not look like a one-off event. It had enough violence, enough police dispersal, and enough political symbolism to generate follow-up activity. I expect at least one more organized demonstration or counter-demonstration within the next week, most likely in The Hague but possibly also in Amsterdam.

What I would expect:

A smaller far-right march than before, but still visible.
Counter-protests from anti-fascist, left-wing, and pro-immigration groups.
Heavy police presence, especially near government buildings, embassies, or symbolic spots in The Hague.
Some chance of clashes, not necessarily a major riot, but enough for media clips of water cannons, tear gas, arrests, or broken windows.
Parliamentary questions from opposition parties asking why the protest was allowed to get that far, whether permits were handled properly, and whether the government is being too soft or too hard on immigration protests.
The political framing will be important. The cabinet will probably say something like: “the right to protest remains, but violence and antisemitic slogans are not acceptable.” Right-wing parties may claim the street is showing what many voters feel about immigration. Left-wing parties will use the violence to argue that far-right mobilization is becoming more dangerous.

This protest wave could also be fed by other stories in your list, especially the US/Afrikaner visa debate and the general anti-immigration mood. If another rally is announced quickly, I would not be surprised if it draws a few thousand people rather than just a few hundred.

Railway disruption remains a live problem; farmer pressure stays high
Likelihood: medium-high
The railway sabotage around Prinsjesdag was not just an inconvenience. It happened on one of the most symbolically important political days of the year, and the fact that farmers are being considered as a possible motive makes this politically sensitive. I expect the investigation to continue through the week, and I would not be surprised if there is at least one more disruption or near-disruption, even if smaller than the initial wave.

What I predict:

NS will keep extra staff on trains, but delays will remain a public irritant.
Police will increase checks around rail infrastructure, especially in areas with active farmer protests or recent incidents.
Farmer organizations may use the sabotage story to renew pressure on the nitrogen policy, even if they deny direct responsibility for all of it.
There could be tractor actions, road blockades, or symbolic demonstrations near major highways, Schiphol, The Hague, or Utrecht.
The government will be pushed to say whether this was local farmer anger, a hybrid operation, or something in between. It may avoid giving a clean answer because the evidence is still developing.
The key political risk is that transport chaos becomes another argument against the cabinet’s budget and nitrogen plans. If trains keep running badly while the government talks about taxes and reforms, public patience will drop.

My read: the Netherlands will not be “paralyzed,” but transport reliability will remain a visible weakness all week. If a second major sabotage incident hits energy, digital infrastructure, or Schiphol, then this story jumps from annoying to serious.

Capital gains tax and post-Prinsjesdag politics will get louder
Likelihood: medium-high
The budget season is only beginning, but the capital gains tax announcement already gives the opposition and business groups something concrete to attack. I expect more details, leaks, or official clarifications during this week.

What to watch:

The cabinet will try to present the capital gains levy as a modernization of the Dutch tax system, not a new tax windfall.
Business associations, investors, real estate groups, and savings-focused lobbies will react sharply.
Coalition partners may show different levels of comfort with the reform. This is the kind of fiscal issue that can create internal tension if the government wants to keep the plan credible but also avoid a market scare.
Dutch media will frame it as “new taxes” or “wealth tax pressure,” even if the government says it is about alignment with the rest of Europe.
The likely political effect is not an immediate cabinet crisis, but a souring of public mood. People are already dealing with transport disruption, cost-of-living pressure, and immigration debates. Add tax reform talk into that mix and the government will feel more heat than usual.

Xavi’s Netherlands will play official Nations League football, and the result will matter culturally
Likelihood of at least one win: high
Likelihood of a strong overall result from two games: medium-high
The new coach story is important because Dutch football always becomes a national mood indicator. The omission of Memphis Depay, the continuation of Virgil van Dijk, and the call-up of Ruben van Bommel are already giving the media plenty to discuss. If Xavi’s team plays its first official Nations League match or matches this week, I expect the result to be decent but not flawless.

My prediction:

The Netherlands wins at least one game.
A realistic winning scoreline is 2-0, 3-1, or 1-0.
If they play twice, there is a real chance of one win and one draw or narrow loss.
The team will look more controlled than exciting. Xavi’s emphasis on intensity and initiative will be tested against opponents who sit deep and hit on the counter.
Depay’s absence will remain a talking point, especially if the Netherlands struggle to create chances.
Van Dijk’s continued presence will be framed as leadership and possibly as one of his final international cycles.
If the Netherlands win convincingly, it gives the country a useful distraction from politics and transport problems. If they drop points badly, Xavi will face immediate tactical criticism, especially around pressing, transitions, and whether the squad is too conservative.

Klaas Knot and the ECB story stays in the background but gains momentum
Likelihood of a formal decision this week: low
Likelihood of more lobbying and media speculation: medium
The France/Knot angle is not something that will resolve in one week, but it will keep generating European financial press. I expect:

French officials or allies to continue signaling support for Knot.
Dutch officials to remain publicly cautious while privately encouraging the candidacy.
More speculation about whether the chief economist role could be used as part of a broader package.
No formal ECB announcement unless something unexpected happens around Lagarde’s 2027 departure timeline.
This story matters because it shows the Netherlands still has serious influence in European finance, even if domestic politics is noisy. It may also gain relevance if the ICC sanctions create extra stress around European financial infrastructure.

The Iran-linked spyware advisory will have a quiet follow-up
Likelihood of major public incident: low
Likelihood of official follow-up or institutional checks: medium
The UK-US-Netherlands advisory on Iranian spyware is not likely to become a mass-media story unless a specific Dutch ministry, international organization, or prominent target is confirmed. But The Hague is full of international institutions, so the stakes are higher than in many countries.

What I expect:

The Dutch NCSC may ask agencies and organizations to check systems.
There could be a follow-up statement urging vigilance.
If an Iranian opposition figure, journalist, NGO, or international body based in the Netherlands is found to have been targeted, the story will expand quickly.
If nothing concrete is revealed, it remains a specialist cybersecurity issue rather than a mainstream political one.
This is one of those stories that could become much bigger with a single confirmation. Without that, it stays under the radar.

Dutch tech and finance will get some positive attention
Likelihood: medium-low, but directionally positive
The Euclyd/Euclide AI chip funding round, Nexperia’s Tata Electronics partnership, and Main Capital’s Watermark Insights deal are not going to dominate daily news, but they give the Dutch government and business press a useful counter-narrative: the Netherlands is still attracting serious investment in chips, AI, and corporate finance.

What I expect:

More press coverage of Dutch semiconductor strength.
Government officials using these deals to argue for continued support of the tech ecosystem.
Business media focusing on how the Netherlands is positioning itself between European regulation and global capital.
Possibly another investment announcement or partnership in the same space, because once this theme starts moving, more companies tend to follow.
This will not fix the political mood by itself, but it helps the government claim that the country is still economically relevant.

Culture and sports add color, but they are secondary
Likelihood of minor media attention: high
Likelihood of major national impact: low
Several smaller stories will keep circulating:

The Van Gogh exhibition at the Kröller-Müller museum will draw large visitor numbers. Expect more coverage of record attendance, travel to Otterlo, and local economic benefits.
Luke Littler’s threat to boycott Dutch darts events after being booed in Amsterdam will generate a PDC response and fan debate. I would expect some kind of official statement or mediation, but not a full collapse of the Dutch calendar. He may skip one event, but a complete boycott seems less likely unless the incident escalates.
The stolen Earl Spencer Diana book will remain a curiosity story. Police may trace the truck route or find the copies, but this is unlikely to become a major national issue.
The 2030 Winter Olympics speed skating allocation for the Netherlands will get some local and regional attention, especially around venue planning and infrastructure expectations.
These items matter less politically, but they keep the country visible in international culture and sport.

Most likely sequence for the week:

Early week:
The ICC sanctions story dominates. Dutch and EU officials sound alarms, make calls with Washington, and prepare public messaging. The cabinet tries to avoid looking either too emotional or too submissive.

Midweek:
Protest activity becomes more visible if another rally is organized. At the same time, transport disruption and farmer pressure keep the government on the back foot. Budget and capital gains tax talk gets more concrete in parliament and business media.

Weekend:
Football becomes the main national distraction. If Xavi’s Netherlands performs well, public mood improves. If they underperform, the sports press gives the coach a hard time. There is also a chance of one more protest-related incident or a follow-up transport disruption if conditions remain tense.

What would change my forecast:

If the US delays ICC sanctions beyond this week, the domestic protest and budget stories become relatively bigger.
If another major sabotage hit occurs on energy, digital infrastructure, or Schiphol, security becomes the main story.
If a far-right protest turns into serious violence, parliamentary pressure on the cabinet increases sharply.
If Xavi’s team loses badly, football criticism becomes louder, but it still does not change the political center of gravity.
If a specific Dutch institution or minister is confirmed as targeted by Iranian spyware, the cybersecurity story jumps up quickly.
My overall read:

The Netherlands will not be in a full crisis next week, but it will look like a country dealing with several small shocks at once. The government’s best outcome is to contain the ICC sanctions fallout, keep transport mostly moving, avoid another ugly protest night, and let football provide a distraction.

If I had to summarize it in one sentence:
The Netherlands will spend the next week trying to look stable while Washington pressures its most important international court, domestic protesters test the government’s nerves, farmers keep infrastructure politics alive, and Xavi is judged on whether his new Netherlands can perform under pressure.
