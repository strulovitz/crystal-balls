1. Politics: Merz stays, but the center gets squeezed
Most likely political development
Merz will survive the immediate post-election shock. He has already said he is staying, and there is no sign yet of a formal leadership challenge or confidence vote. The next few days will be about damage control, coalition management, and party discipline.

Expect:

CDU/CSU internal meetings
Pressure from state premiers and party hardliners
Demands that Merz show a clearer policy direction
More talk of “reforms,” “stability,” and “responsibility”
A possible cabinet reshuffle or new task force, but not necessarily a full government collapse
The most likely outcome is that Merz uses the next week to consolidate his position by announcing some kind of reform package or stability agenda. He will probably frame the election result as painful but not fatal, and he will try to convert it into a mandate for difficult reforms: energy security, industrial competitiveness, migration management, fiscal discipline, and defense readiness.

The real danger: CDU infighting
The bigger risk is not an immediate Bundestag crisis. It is internal erosion inside the CDU/CSU. If enough state party leaders or parliamentary group members start believing that Merz is too weak to carry the coalition, then the next few weeks could get much uglier.

For the next seven days, I would expect:

Strong public support from loyalists
Private grumbling from eastern CDU figures
More aggressive language from AfD about Merz’s “twilight” or weakening grip
Possible speculation about a leadership contest later in autumn, but not necessarily an immediate vote
So: Merz stays this week. His authority is damaged but not broken.

State government formation will be the next big political story
This is probably the most concrete thing that will happen in Germany in the next week.

Berlin
The Left party’s win in Berlin under Elif Eralp will likely lead to coalition talks for a new state government. The most plausible direction is a left-leaning state government, likely involving the Left and at least one of SPD or Greens, possibly both.

That creates an interesting dynamic:

At federal level, SPD and Greens are in coalition with Merz’s CDU/CSU
At Berlin state level, they may now be working with the Left
This will not immediately break the federal government, but it will create some awkwardness. SPD and Greens will have to explain why they can support a left-led Berlin government while also supporting Merz federally.

Mecklenburg-Vorpommern
This is likely to be messier. If the AfD becomes the strongest party or a key player in the new state parliament, then older parties will face the question of whether to work with them, contain them, or form a minority/caretaker arrangement.

Possible scenarios:

AfD seeks a governing role directly
AfD forms a coalition with one or more smaller parties
Older parties try to build a government excluding AfD but still dependent on its support
Talks drag on, leaving the state in political limbo for weeks
My read: Berlin will probably move faster toward a workable left-leaning government. Mecklenburg-Vorpommern will be the harder case and may become a symbolic test of how Germany handles the AfD at the state level.

The AfD–Kremlin gas story could become a major news driver
One of the most interesting headlines is that Putin’s economic envoy and AfD leadership are preparing talks about getting Russian gas back for Germany. This is not just an energy story. It is a political identity story.

In the next week, I would expect one or more of the following:

A meeting actually takes place
A leak appears in the media
Photos or statements surface
The government is forced to respond publicly
Opposition parties demand explanations about how close AfD and the Kremlin are on energy policy
Merz’s government will probably say something like: this is not an official government channel, but it is relevant to Germany’s energy interests. The CDU/CSU base may not hate that message as much as one would expect, especially in eastern Germany, where gas prices and industrial competitiveness matter a lot.

This story matters because it gives the AfD a way to say:
“We are not just protest voters. We are capable of real policy talks.”

That strengthens their claim to be a serious governing force, even if they do not immediately form a federal government.

2. Energy and economy: gas storage, fuel relief, and market nerves
Gas storage incentives will likely become official
The German government is already planning market incentives to keep more gas available for winter. In the next week, I would expect this to move from “source says” or “planning stage” to something more concrete:

expanded tender
payments to traders/suppliers to keep gas in storage
possibly additional guarantees or price mechanisms
public messaging about winter readiness
This is one of the most likely policy announcements of the week. It gives the government a visible action it can point to after the election shock.

Fuel relief measures are also likely, but maybe not fully detailed yet
If fuel prices remain high because of the Iran conflict and Hormuz pressure, the government will face pressure to do something for households, transport companies, logistics firms, and industry.

Possible measures:

temporary VAT relief on petrol/diesel
compensation for transport and logistics companies
targeted support for heating costs
emergency coordination between economy, finance, and transport ministries
a package of short-term relief measures rather than one big symbolic cut
I would not be surprised if the next week brings either:

an official announcement, or
a very strong signal that something is coming within days
The political pressure is too high to do nothing while fuel prices stay elevated.

The economy will show weakness in Q3, but the government will still talk about year-end recovery
The Bundesbank and other data suggest the economy loses momentum in the third quarter before possibly picking up toward year-end. In the next week, surveys and market commentary will probably keep saying:

confidence is weak
industry is under pressure
politics is adding uncertainty
but there are still structural reforms underway
Merz’s government will want to use that narrative:
“Yes, it is hard now, but we are doing the difficult reforms, and recovery is coming.”

The problem is that if auto workers are protesting, bond yields are high, and property funds are under stress, that message may not land very well.

Bond markets will remain a major nerve center
German Bund yields hitting a 15-year high is important. This means the market is no longer treating Germany as an automatic safe haven with zero political risk premium. It is now pricing in:

fiscal pressure
energy costs
industrial weakness
political instability
possible inflation persistence
In the next week, I would expect:

elevated volatility in German bonds
continued pressure on government borrowing costs
more commentary about whether central banks can keep ignoring inflation
nervousness around any new fuel/energy price shock
If fuel prices spike further, bond yields could move higher still. That would make Merz’s job even harder because it ties domestic politics to global financial markets.

Property fund stress could become a real financial story
The DWS / Deutsche Bank property fund liquidity issue is one of the more important “quiet” stories in this set of headlines. If large German property funds face redemption pressure, that can quickly become a broader confidence problem.

In the next week, I would expect:

possible announcements about liquidity buffers
possible curbs on redemptions
more media focus on German real estate stress
questions about whether banks are exposed
This is not yet a bank crisis, but it is the kind of story that can turn into one if communication is bad. If DWS or another major manager announces emergency measures, that will dominate financial headlines for a day or two.

3. Industry and labor: auto protests and VW pain will keep dominating
Auto workers will likely keep protesting
The VW profit warning, Porsche write-down, cost-cutting acceleration, and worker protests are all connected. This is not just one company story. It is a German industrial policy crisis.

In the next week, I would expect:

further demonstrations
possible strike action at VW, BMW, Mercedes, or Bosch sites
works councils pressing for job protection, shift guarantees, and transition support
management defending cost cuts as necessary for survival
government trying to mediate without looking like it is bailing out industry
The most likely industrial event of the week is another major protest action or symbolic strike in the auto sector, probably with VW at the center.

VW will remain the symbol of Germany’s industrial stress
Volkswagen is now a political issue, not just a corporate issue. The next week could bring:

more details on cost-cutting
plant or shift discussions
wage negotiations under pressure
possible leadership statements about European production and EV transition
more media focus on whether German car plants are secure
If VW announces something major — a plant decision, shift reduction, or restructuring package — it will immediately become national political news.

The EU may start moving against Chinese EV competition
Germany’s finance minister urging EU action against China to defend carmakers is another sign that Berlin is getting more aggressive on industrial policy.

In the next week, I would expect:

more German pressure on Brussels
possible discussion of anti-subsidy measures
talk of local content rules, tariffs, or “fair competition” frameworks for EVs and batteries
coordination with France and other auto-producing EU states
But I would not expect a final major EU decision in just seven days. More likely: signals, consultations, and pressure building toward a later package.

4. Security and foreign policy: Germany will act more alert, but still domestically constrained
Expect a higher security posture
The stories about hospitals preparing for possible casualties, a drone startup boss living in hiding, and Russia possibly striking Ukraine’s allies suggest that Germany is taking the threat environment more seriously.

In the next week, I would expect:

more attention to energy infrastructure security
tighter protection around defense firms and critical tech companies
possible government briefings on civil protection readiness
more discussion of hospitals, shelters, stockpiles, and resilience
There may not be a major incident, but the tone will be more serious. Germany is moving from “we are rearming” to “we are also preparing for disruption.”

A Russian drone/plane incident would change the week dramatically
If a Russian military aircraft or drone appears near the Baltic, over Poland, or in an area that involves German airspace/interests, it will dominate the news cycle. That is the main security wildcard.

If it happens:

Germany will likely call it a hostile signal
NATO allies will respond quickly
Berlin may increase air policing or defensive posture
Merz’s government will have to show strength without looking overreactive
If it does not happen, the security story will still be present but less dramatic.

NATO and Bundeswehr announcements will continue
With a German general elected to head NATO’s top military body and Germany receiving its first F-35, the next week will likely include more defense-related messaging:

rearmament progress
European autonomy
integration of new equipment
stronger deterrence against Russia
This is useful for Merz because it gives his government a strong, unifying theme. Defense spending and readiness are areas where broad political support is still possible, even if the economy is weak.

The EU budget issue will become more uncertain
Merz’s political weakness now affects the EU agenda, especially the €2tn budget/MFF deal. Brussels will be nervous because Germany is central to that package.

In the next week, I would expect:

German ministers signaling support but asking for flexibility
Brussels becoming more cautious about timing
more talk of “months of uncertainty” if Merz’s position weakens further
The most likely outcome is not an immediate collapse of the EU deal, but slowing down and hedging language.

UNGA: Merz will probably stay home or send a deputy
Given that he already canceled his UN trip amid party infighting, it would be surprising if he suddenly went to New York in the middle of this domestic crisis. More likely:

Germany is represented by the foreign minister or another senior official
Merz stays in Berlin to manage CDU/coalition politics
German diplomacy focuses on Ukraine, Iran/Hormuz, energy security, and possibly AI governance
This absence will be noted. It reinforces the image of a government under domestic pressure.

In the next week:

if Germany wins, it will be used as evidence that the country can still perform and find unity
if Germany draws or loses, critics will say it reflects national confusion, lack of identity, and leadership weakness
This is not just football. It becomes a public mood event. A win could give Merz and the government a small morale boost. A loss would feed the narrative that Germany is struggling on multiple fronts at once.

Oktoberfest and public life will continue, but with a cost-of-living overlay
Oktoberfest is running, beer prices are rising, flu jabs are being offered, and Frankfurt’s malaria cluster has become a minor public health story. None of these will determine the political week, but they will shape everyday public mood.

Expect:

security measures at Oktoberfest
more talk about beer prices and inflation
travel/health guidance around the Frankfurt malaria cases
a general sense that normal life continues even as politics gets tense
These stories matter because they remind people that the crisis is not only abstract. It shows up in beer prices, fuel costs, airport health scares, and public gatherings.

What would break this forecast?
The main scenarios that would change my read are:

1. CDU turns on Merz faster than expected
If the CDU/CSU parliamentary group or enough state leaders start openly questioning his leadership, then the week shifts from “Merz survives” to “Merz is in real danger.” That would raise the odds of a reshuffle, a policy U-turn, or a leadership contest soon.

2. A major auto strike shuts key production
If VW or another major employer moves beyond protests into serious work stoppages, the economic story becomes much more urgent. Then the government may be forced into emergency industrial mediation or support measures.

3. Fuel prices spike sharply
If crude/gasoline/diesel prices jump because of Hormuz or Iran-related escalation, the government may have to move faster on relief and energy security. That would make the week more crisis-driven.

4. A Russian security incident happens near Germany/Baltic region
A drone, plane, or other military incident would immediately raise the defense/security story to the top of the agenda and change the tone of Merz’s government from domestic reform to external deterrence.

5. DWS property fund stress turns into a broader financial scare
If major German property funds announce redemption curbs or liquidity problems in a way that worries banks and investors, the economic story shifts from “weak growth” to “financial stress.” That would pressure the government much harder.
