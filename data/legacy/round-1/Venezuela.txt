Short answer: the next week is likely to be dominated by New York diplomacy, oil/gold money deals, and María Corina Machado’s return question. Venezuela probably will not “explode” into a full domestic crisis in the next 7 days, but it will look more visibly US-managed, with Delcy Rodríguez trying to trade oil, gold, and political gestures for legitimacy while keeping the security apparatus intact.

Assuming “next week” means roughly Sept 20–27, 2026, here is my straight read.

Most likely things to happen in the next week
1. Delcy Rodríguez goes to New York and meets Trump / senior US officials
This is the centerpiece.

The headlines already say Trump will meet her during UN General Assembly week. That meeting will probably be staged as a major normalization moment: photo op, joint statement, or at least a public signal that the US is backing Rodríguez’s interim government.

What I expect from that meeting:

A strong US message that Venezuela is “back” in the Western economic orbit.
Focus on oil investment, sanctions relief, gold reserves, and a vague political roadmap.
Trump framing it as a win: stability, oil access, reduced drug transit, Latin American influence.
Rodríguez trying to get explicit US backing against Machado and against internal Chavista doubts.
Likelihood: very high.

What the outcome probably looks like: Not a full “we solved Venezuela” announcement, but enough public language to make markets feel reassured. The political transition timetable will likely remain fuzzy unless Trump and Rodríguez agree on a concrete deadline.

2. The $4 billion gold deal moves from “nearing” to announced or near-announced
This is one of the biggest stories in your list.

The FT, Reuters, and others say Venezuela is nearing a deal to move roughly $4 billion in gold from London / Bank of England custody to New York or US-linked custody, with an opposition faction involved. That is politically explosive because it touches sovereignty, transition financing, and who controls the state’s assets.

What I expect next week:

Either a formal announcement of an agreement in principle.
Or a statement that technical / legal arrangements are being finalized.
Possible involvement of US financial actors, opposition representatives, and maybe Heeney Capital or similar intermediaries.
Domestic controversy from both sides:
Chavistas may say the gold is being handed to the US.
Opposition moderates may say it is necessary collateral for transition.
Machado’s camp may attack it as a deal made without full democratic legitimacy.
Likelihood of some public advance on the gold deal: high.

Full physical transfer completed in one week? Less likely. But a major announcement or near-final framework? Plausible.

Why this matters: If the US and an opposition faction can help Rodríguez access that gold, it gives her fiscal breathing room. It also makes the US more directly entangled in Venezuela’s transition. That is exactly the kind of move that creates both support and resentment at home.

3. Oil deals accelerate, especially Exxon, Continental, Total, and possibly Chevron
The oil story is already moving fast:

Exxon is reportedly nearing a preliminary deal.
Harold Hamm’s Continental Resources has signed or is announcing a preliminary deal.
TotalEnergies signed an MoU.
The US seems to be pushing a broader framework that could include a direct US government stake and a 2 million barrels/day target.
Next week, I would expect:

More oil company announcements or “preliminary agreements.”
Possible follow-on interest from Chevron, private operators, trading houses, and infrastructure firms.
More public language about lifting sanctions enough to let Western companies operate safely.
Senate Democrats pressing the Trump administration to explain what happened to the $13 billion in Venezuelan oil revenue.
Likelihood of more oil-related announcements: high.

Likelihood of a fully binding mega-contract being signed and explained in detail within one week: medium.

The bigger story is not one single contract. It is the visible return of Western capital into Venezuela under US protection. That will dominate markets and domestic politics.

Watch for:

Whether Exxon’s deal becomes “preliminary agreement” rather than just talks.
Whether the US government stake gets clarified.
Whether the $13 billion oil revenue question forces a White House or State Department explanation.
Whether Trump uses Venezuela as a template while talking about Iran / Hormuz / global oil supply.
4. Machado makes a major move: announcement of return, possible landing, or a hard deadline
This is the biggest domestic variable.

The WSJ exclusive says she is planning her comeback and that it will clash with the US-backed interim government. Reuters earlier said she was weighing return amid US reticence. That combination suggests she may use the next week to force the issue.

Three realistic possibilities:

A. She announces a return date but does not land yet
Likelihood: high.

This is the safest move for her. She can build momentum, pressure Rodríguez, and avoid landing in the middle of a delicate New York deal-making window.

B. She actually returns within the next week
Likelihood: medium to medium-high.

If she lands, expect:

Heavy security at Maiquetía airport.
Supporters gathering, probably with some controlled access.
Rodríguez’s government trying to look open and calm.
Possible short public appearance or speech.
No immediate all-out crackdown while US eyes are on her, but targeted pressure on allies, media, or organizers.
Would she be arrested immediately? I would not bet on it if the US is watching closely. More likely: controlled freedom at first, then constraints later if she becomes too disruptive.

C. She delays return until after Rodríguez’s New York trip
Likelihood: medium.

This would make sense if the US wants to avoid two big stories at once: Trump-Rodríguez meeting and Machado landing. But Machado may not want to wait, because waiting lets Rodríguez lock in oil/gold deals first.

My best guess: Machado will use the next week to announce a very near return or actually land. Either way, her name will be central to the domestic story.

5. Government and opposition dialogue continues, with small liberalization gestures
The headlines show a second cycle of dialogue between the government and an opposition faction, with gains on rights and transparency. Venezuela also unblocked some censored news sites, though many remain blocked.

Next week I expect:

More announcements about media access.
Possible additional news sites being unblocked.
Technical talks on electoral transparency.
Maybe some prisoner releases or softer treatment of political detainees as a signal.
A third round of dialogue being scheduled or already taking place.
Likelihood of continued dialogue and small gestures: high.

Likelihood of deep institutional reform in one week: low.

Human Rights Watch saying there is a “pause” in overt repression but that reforms are still needed fits this pattern. The regime is trying to look less harsh without fully dismantling the security state.

What this means politically: Rodríguez can claim moderation and openness. Machado can argue the concessions are too small, too late, and tied to an oil deal. The UN experts can say the repression apparatus remains intact. All three can be true at once.

6. Domestic politics gets more tense around “who is selling Venezuela?”
The next week will likely produce a lot of domestic argument over:

Oil deals.
Gold reserves.
US control or influence.
Dollarization.
Whether Rodríguez has legitimacy.
Whether elections are being delayed.
Chavistas may complain that the country is being handed to Washington and Wall Street. Opposition moderates may defend the deals as necessary for economic survival. Machado’s camp may attack both sides: the government for selling assets, and moderate opposition for legitimizing an interim government too quickly.

Likelihood of public political fights over oil/gold: high.

Likelihood of this becoming a mass street movement in one week: medium.

The main domestic narrative will probably be: “Venezuela is getting money and oil investment, but at what political cost?”

7. Economy: dollarization debate grows, but no formal adoption next week
The Bloomberg pieces show dollarization moving into the mainstream debate. With gold, oil revenue, aluminum shipments, and US financial involvement all in play, there will be more talk about anchoring the economy to the dollar.

Next week:

More public debate.
Possible technical discussions.
Maybe statements from economists, central bank figures, or opposition politicians.
But I would not expect formal dollarization to be announced in seven days.
Likelihood of major dollarization announcement next week: low to medium-low.

Likelihood of it becoming a prominent political issue: high.

The government may like the idea because it could reduce inflation expectations and make oil/gold revenue more usable. But formal adoption is a big move, and they will probably want more control over the narrative first.

8. Security forces stay alert but probably avoid a major visible crackdown
The UN says the repression apparatus remains largely intact. HRW says there is a pause in overt repression. That suggests the security state is still there, just less noisy for now.

Next week:

More intelligence monitoring around Machado’s return and oil/gold protests.
Possible selective detentions of low-profile activists or organizers.
Internet or social media pressure if crowds grow.
Security forces trying to show control without looking brutal while the US is watching.
Likelihood of a major crackdown in the next 7 days: medium-low, unless Machado returns and something goes wrong.

Likelihood of targeted pressure on opposition figures: high.

The regime’s goal will be to look calm enough for New York diplomacy while keeping enough force behind it to prevent loss of control.

Less likely but possible events in the next week
A sudden election date is announced
Possible, but I would rate it medium-low.

The headlines say short-term elections have been put on hold because of the oil deal and legitimacy questions. That suggests Rodríguez and Washington prefer stabilization first. But if Machado returns and creates pressure, they might announce a date to take the wind out of her sails.

If an election date is announced next week, that would be a major surprise and would immediately reshape the story.

A bigger opposition split becomes public
Likely in some form.

The gold deal involving “a faction of the opposition” already suggests the opposition is not unified. Next week may see:

One opposition group defending the deal.
Another accusing it of premature compromise.
Machado positioning herself as the leader who refuses to accept a US-backed interim government without a clearer democratic path.
This split will matter more than any single protest.

A military or palace drama emerges
Possible, but I would rate it low for one week.

Oil and gold deals can create winners and losers inside the Chavista camp. If Rodríguez looks too close to Washington, some hardliners may grumble. But a full military split in seven days seems less likely unless something shocks them: Machado returning with mass support, a bad oil revenue disclosure, or a visible US demand for rapid elections.

The $13 billion oil revenue question becomes a congressional fight
This is fairly likely to get louder.

Senate Democrats are already demanding an accounting. Next week could bring:

A formal letter.
A request for documents.
A hearing announcement.
Or at least pressure on the State Department to explain how much oil money was collected and how much went back to Caracas.
This will matter because it affects whether Venezuelans see the US as a partner or as a creditor extracting value.

What I think is the most probable overall outcome by end of next week
By the end of the next seven days, Venezuela will probably look like this:

Rodríguez has been in New York and met Trump or senior US officials.
The US publicly frames Venezuela as a success story of oil diplomacy and regional stabilization.
The gold deal is either announced as an agreement in principle or described as imminent.
One or more oil companies make new public announcements about entering or preparing to enter Venezuela.
Machado has either returned, announced a return date very close at hand, or set conditions that force Rodríguez to respond.
The government makes additional small liberalization gestures: more media unblocking, dialogue progress, maybe some prisoner releases.
The opposition is visibly divided between those willing to work with the interim government and those led by Machado demanding a faster democratic transition.
The security apparatus remains strong but tries to stay less visible than under Maduro.
No full domestic explosion yet, but the political tension rises because the oil/gold deals make the stakes concrete.
In one line: Next week is likely a US-backed normalization week: New York photo ops, oil and gold announcements, small democratic gestures, and Machado pressing the return-home button.

The key things to watch in real time
Watch these first, because they will tell you which scenario is winning:

Does Rodríguez meet Trump in person? If yes, the normalization story becomes official.
What exactly do they say about elections? A date changes everything. Vagueness means Rodríguez is buying time.
Is the gold deal announced as “transfer,” “custody,” “escrow,” or “US account”? The wording will reveal how much control the US and opposition faction gain.
Does Machado land, or does she set a landing date? If she lands, domestic volatility rises sharply. If she sets a date, Rodríguez has to respond publicly.
Does Exxon make a preliminary deal public? That would be a major signal that the oil opening is real, not just political theater.
Do Democrats force an explanation on the $13 billion? If the US kept or spent a large share without clear accounting, domestic anger could grow.
How many more media sites are unblocked? This will be used as proof of “progress” by Washington and Rodríguez, and as evidence of “too little, too late” by Machado’s camp.
Bottom line prediction
If I had to give you the single most likely next-week scenario:

Rodríguez goes to New York, meets Trump, and comes home with a public US endorsement of the oil/gold stabilization plan. The gold deal moves closer to announcement. Oil companies keep making preliminary moves. Machado uses the moment to announce her return or actually returns, forcing Rodríguez to balance US-backed economic deals against the democratic pressure Machado represents. The government makes small opening gestures but keeps the security state intact. No full crisis in seven days, but the fight over Venezuela’s future becomes much more concrete.

The core tension of the next week is this: Can Rodríguez turn oil and gold into enough legitimacy to delay elections, or will Machado’s return make that delay impossible to sell domestically?
