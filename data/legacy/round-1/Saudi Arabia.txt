I’ll be blunt: the headlines describe Saudi Arabia entering a high-stress week where the main problems are Houthi missile/drone attacks, damaged oil infrastructure, strained air defenses, and a scramble for allies. I’m assuming “next week” means roughly the seven days after 21 September 2026.

Short verdict
Saudi Arabia will spend the next week in damage-control mode. It will not suddenly collapse, but it will be under continuous pressure from Yemen and the Red Sea. The most likely outcome is: more Houthi attacks on Saudi targets, more Saudi airstrikes into Yemen, partial restoration of the damaged oil pipeline, continued rerouting of crude via Hormuz/Oman, and a diplomatic push by MBS to get air-defense help from the US, UK, Turkey, Egypt, Pakistan, and Gulf partners.

The kingdom’s biggest near-term risk is not one single attack. It is the combination of:

Houthi fire rate outpacing Saudi interceptor supplies
Oil export disruption becoming longer than officials want to admit
The Red Sea/Bab el-Mandeb route staying dangerous
Domestic and religious sensitivity around Riyadh, Mecca, Medina, Jeddah, and Yanbu
What I expect will happen in the next 7 days
1. More Houthi missile and drone attacks on Saudi Arabia
I would expect at least two or three more Houthi attack waves against Saudi territory in the next week. The targets are likely to include:

Riyadh, especially around King Khalid International Airport and fuel storage areas
Jeddah
Yanbu
Red Sea port infrastructure
Oil terminals, refineries, airports, or logistics hubs near the coast
The Houthis have already claimed attacks on Riyadh, Mecca-area airspace, Yanbu, and airport fuel depots. That pattern will probably continue. They are trying to show that Saudi Arabia cannot protect its interior, not just its border.

I would not be surprised if at least one attack causes visible damage or minor casualties from falling debris. There has already been one death from intercepted drone debris. In the next week, I expect more civil-defense alerts, all-clears, smoke, flight delays, and official statements saying that missiles or drones were “neutralized” before reaching their targets.

The key question is whether a missile or drone gets close enough to Riyadh, Mecca, Medina, Jeddah, or Yanbu to cause a political shock. If something hits near Mecca or Medina, even if intercepted outside the city, Saudi Arabia will treat it as a major “red line” moment.

2. Saudi Arabia will keep striking Yemen, but probably avoid a full ground invasion for now
Saudi Arabia and its coalition will likely continue daily or near-daily airstrikes against Houthi positions in Yemen. Expected targets:

Drone launch sites
Missile storage areas
Radar installations
Coastal batteries
Port facilities near the Bab el-Mandeb Strait
Houthi logistics nodes
Possibly command-and-control sites closer to Sanaa
The Saudi objective is probably to degrade Houthi firing capability and protect Red Sea oil exports. But I do not think Saudi Arabia will immediately launch a large-scale ground invasion into Houthi-held Yemen in the next week unless one of these happens:

A major Saudi aircraft is shot down again
Mecca or Medina is directly hit
A major tanker or oil terminal suffers a serious attack
The Houthis take more strategic ground near the Red Sea coast
The loss of a Saudi F-15, if confirmed by wreckage and video, will hurt MBS politically. It shows that Houthi air defenses are dangerous. But I still expect Riyadh to prefer air power, precision strikes, special operations, and naval pressure before committing large ground forces. A ground war in Yemen would be messy and could become another long-term drain on Saudi prestige.

3. The East-West oil pipeline will likely restart partially, but not fully
This is one of the most important issues. The damaged East-West pipeline is a major export route to the Red Sea. Officials are saying “days,” while satellite imagery and some reports suggest repairs could take weeks.

My prediction:

Within the next 3 to 5 days, Saudi Arabia will likely restore some capacity, probably around half or somewhat less than half.
Full restoration by the end of the week is less likely unless the damage was less severe than reported.
Saudi Aramco will continue telling customers that some cargoes are delayed, cancelled, or rerouted.
More crude will be moved through alternative routes:
Strait of Hormuz
Ship-to-ship transfers off Oman
Dark shipments
Possible increased loadings from eastern terminals
Saudi Arabia is already reportedly offering more crude via Oman and telling some European refiners they may get no crude next month under long-term contracts. That will continue. The message to customers will be: “We are compensating, but expect friction.”

Oil prices will remain volatile. If the pipeline restarts partially and cargo flows look strong, oil may dip. If a new Houthi attack hits Yanbu, Jeddah, or another export point, oil can spike again quickly. The market is already nervous about 4 million barrels per day of Saudi export capacity being at risk.

4. MBS will intensify the diplomatic scramble
The next week will be dominated by phone calls, emergency diplomacy, and alliance management. I expect MBS or senior Saudi officials to push hard for:

More Patriot/THAAD interceptors from the US
Naval or air support from the UK
Technical military assistance from Turkey
Red Sea security cooperation with Egypt
Pressure on Iran through Pakistan
Regional talks involving Oman, Iran, and Gulf states
The F-35 sale is important symbolically. The US approving 48 F-35 jets for $24.3 billion shows Washington still wants Saudi Arabia in its orbit. But F-35s are not going to save the next week. They are a political signal, not an immediate air-defense fix.

Turkey looks like one of the most useful near-term supporters. Ankara has signaled readiness to meet Saudi defense needs under a trilateral defense arrangement. In practical terms, that could mean:

Air-defense systems or missiles
Drone support
Intelligence sharing
Training
Naval cooperation in the Red Sea
Political cover for a stronger Saudi response
The UK is also considering support, likely because Houthi attacks threaten British economic interests and Red Sea shipping. I would not be surprised if London moves from “mulling” to some visible form of assistance: naval patrols, intelligence, air-defense help, or joint operations.

Egypt is important because the Houthis are pressuring Red Sea routes that matter to Cairo as well. MBS going to Egypt shows Riyadh is trying to build a broader Arab front, not just rely on Washington. Expect more Saudi-Egyptian coordination on:

Red Sea navigation
Port security
Intelligence sharing
Possible joint naval operations
Pakistan may continue pressing Iran to rein in the Houthis, especially because of its defense relationship with Saudi Arabia. But Pakistan is unlikely to force Tehran into a quick compromise. It will mostly serve as a diplomatic pressure valve.

5. The US will want to help but will be careful about direct war
The US is already warning that hostilities could “escalate rapidly.” Washington is also reportedly weighing a larger military operation against the Houthis. But I do not think the US will launch a full-scale operation in the next week unless Saudi Arabia is hit hard again or the Red Sea oil route becomes badly disrupted.

Trump’s administration seems to be balancing:

Supporting MBS and protecting Gulf energy flows
Avoiding another large US military commitment in the Middle East
Using Houthi pressure as leverage in broader Iran negotiations
Linking Saudi security deals to bigger strategic issues, including normalization with Israel and nuclear cooperation
The July headline about the Saudi nuclear deal being tied to Israeli recognition is not necessarily an immediate next-week event, but it matters. If MBS asks Trump for stronger military support, Washington may try to extract diplomatic concessions. Riyadh will be cautious because Arab public opinion is sensitive about Israel, especially if Houthi attacks are making people feel that Saudi Arabia is being dragged into a wider war.

My prediction: the US will provide more air-defense equipment, intelligence, and political backing, but it will still avoid saying it is fully at war with the Houthis or Iran unless a major red line is crossed.

6. Saudi air defenses will be a visible weakness
One of the most important reports is that Saudi Arabia is running low on missile interceptors and has asked allies for help. If that is true, it changes the situation badly. Houthi drones are cheap. Patriot and THAAD missiles are expensive. If Saudi Arabia keeps firing high-value interceptors at low-cost drones, it will face a sustainability problem.

In the next week, expect:

More intercepted attacks, but also more debris falling in populated areas
More official statements about “successful interceptions”
Growing pressure on MBS to show that the kingdom can protect its cities
Possible emergency requests for additional SAM batteries from the US, Gulf states, or Turkey
If another Saudi fighter jet is shot down, or if a Houthi missile hits a major target in Riyadh or Jeddah, the air-defense shortage will become a domestic political issue. The public message will need to be very strong: “We are protected, but we need allies to help us sustain our defenses.”

7. Domestic security and religious sensitivity will rise
Saudi Arabia has already issued wide-ranging security alerts, including around Mecca. That is politically delicate. The Houthis know that attacking near the holy cities creates maximum pressure.

In the next week, I expect:

Tighter security in Riyadh, Jeddah, Mecca, Medina, and Yanbu
More civil-defense warnings and all-clears
Increased patrols around airports, fuel depots, ports, and government areas
Possible temporary flight disruptions at King Khalid International Airport and other major hubs
Greater sensitivity around pilgrim security, even if it is not peak Hajj season
If a drone or missile is intercepted near Mecca or Medina, Saudi Arabia will likely call it a “red line” violation. If debris injures people near holy sites, the reaction could be stronger than if the same thing happened in Riyadh. The religious dimension matters because it affects not just Saudis but the broader Muslim world.

8. The Saudi stock market and oil markets will stay nervous
Saudi stocks have already fallen after Houthi claims about Riyadh. I expect the market to remain weak or highly volatile in the next week. The main drivers will be:

New attack headlines
Pipeline repair updates
Crude cargo cancellations
Shipping insurance costs
Red Sea security news
US and ally support announcements
Oil prices are already elevated, with reports of Brent/US crude moving around $105 to $109. The market is trying to decide whether the Saudi pipeline outage is a short disruption or a longer supply shock. If partial restart happens quickly, oil may cool. If another attack hits export infrastructure, oil can jump again.

Aramco will likely become a key communication channel. Expect more customer calls, cargo adjustments, and reassurances about alternative loading points. The company will be trying to prevent panic among refineries and traders.

9. Saudi Arabia will try to show it is not isolated
One of MBS’s biggest problems is the perception that Saudi Arabia is being squeezed by Iran-aligned forces while Western support is slow or conditional. The TV5 headline asking “Has the US abandoned Saudi Arabia?” captures that anxiety.

In the next week, Riyadh will probably try to demonstrate that it has multiple partners:

US for air defense and F-35s
UK for naval/Red Sea support
Turkey for technical military help
Egypt for Arab Red Sea security cooperation
Pakistan for pressure on Iran
Oman for back-channel diplomacy
Gulf states for financial and logistical support
The goal is to show that Saudi Arabia is not alone. If MBS can get even one visible commitment from a major ally in the next few days, it will help calm domestic nerves.

10. The Houthi front in Yemen will keep expanding pressure on Saudi Arabia
The Houthis are not just firing missiles. They have also been advancing on the Red Sea coast and taking ground overlooking the Bab el-Mandeb Strait. That is strategically serious because it threatens shipping and gives them more room to operate.

In the next week, I expect:

More Houthi claims of territorial gains in Yemen
More attacks on coastal targets
More threats against countries that join Saudi Arabia
Possible targeting of commercial shipping in the Red Sea
Continued pressure on Egypt if Cairo moves closer to Riyadh militarily
The Houthis will try to turn this into a war of attrition. Their logic is simple: they do not need to defeat Saudi Arabia completely. They just need to keep hitting oil, airports, ports, and cities until the political cost becomes too high for Riyadh.

Rough shape of the next 7 days
First 1 to 2 days
More Houthi attacks or at least credible threats against Saudi targets.
Saudi civil-defense alerts in Riyadh, Jeddah, Yanbu, or Mecca-area zones.
Saudi airstrikes on Yemen continue.
Diplomatic calls between MBS, Trump, UK, Turkey, Egypt, and Pakistan intensify.
Oil markets react sharply to any new attack or pipeline news.
Middle of the week
Possible partial restart of part of the East-West pipeline.
More visible ally support announcements, especially from Turkey or the UK.
Saudi Arabia may announce additional air-defense assets or interceptor shipments.
Aramco continues rerouting crude and managing customer expectations.
Houthi attacks on Red Sea shipping become a bigger concern.
End of the week
Saudi Arabia will likely claim that defenses are holding, even if attacks continue.
The pipeline may be partially working but not fully restored.
MBS will have secured at least some visible diplomatic or military support.
Oil prices will remain high and unstable.
The main question will be whether the next Houthi wave hits a more symbolic target: Riyadh, Mecca, Medina, Jeddah, or a major oil facility.
Most likely outcome by confidence level
High likelihood: 70 to 80 percent
Saudi Arabia survives the week with continued attacks, continued retaliation, partial pipeline recovery, and diplomatic support from at least one major ally. The crisis remains serious but contained. Oil stays volatile. No full ground war in Yemen yet.

Medium likelihood: 30 to 45 percent
A more serious Houthi attack hits a major Saudi target, such as Riyadh, Jeddah, Yanbu, or near Mecca/Medina. This causes higher casualties, bigger airport disruptions, and stronger Saudi retaliation. The US may move closer to direct military action against the Houthis.

Lower likelihood: 15 to 25 percent
Major escalation happens quickly: a Saudi jet is lost again, a major tanker is hit, Iran directly strikes Saudi oil infrastructure, or the pipeline repair fails badly. In that case, the next week could turn into a much wider regional crisis.

What would change my forecast
I would revise my prediction upward toward serious escalation if any of these happen:

A Houthi missile or drone hits Mecca or Medina directly.
A major Saudi aircraft is shot down and the pilot is killed or captured.
A large tanker is hit in the Red Sea or Bab el-Mandeb.
Yanbu suffers a major fire or explosion.
The East-West pipeline restart fails and more export capacity is lost.
The US announces direct strikes on Houthi targets.
Iran directly attacks Saudi oil infrastructure.
I would revise my prediction downward toward stabilization if:

Saudi Arabia quickly restores most pipeline capacity.
A major ally delivers a large package of interceptors or air-defense support.
Oman brokers a pause in Houthi attacks.
Turkey and Egypt visibly commit to Red Sea security operations.
The Houthis shift from attacks on cities to targeting only border or port areas.
What is less likely in the next week
I do not expect, within just seven days:

A full-scale Saudi ground invasion of Yemen
A sudden collapse of MBS’s position
Complete restoration of the damaged pipeline
Delivery of F-35s to Saudi Arabia
A formal Saudi-Iran ceasefire without Houthi agreement
A complete end to Red Sea shipping risks
A quick resolution of the US nuclear/Israel recognition issue
Bottom line
The next week in Saudi Arabia will probably look like this: continuous Houthi pressure, Saudi airstrikes into Yemen, partial oil-pipeline recovery, emergency diplomacy, and a market that stays nervous. MBS will be trying to avoid two bad outcomes at once: looking weak domestically, and being dragged into a wider war with Iran-backed forces.

The most realistic picture is not total crisis, but controlled bleeding. Saudi Arabia will keep firing back, keep asking allies for interceptors, keep rerouting oil, and keep telling the public that the kingdom is secure. But if Houthi attacks continue to hit Riyadh, Jeddah, Yanbu, or the holy cities, the pressure on MBS will rise fast.
