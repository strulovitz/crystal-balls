My unfiltered forecast for Israel over the next 7 days — roughly Oct 2 to Oct 9, 2026:

The big picture:
Israel is going to be caught between two security shocks and an election: the FlyDubai cockpit attack and the Oct. 7 warning leaks. Netanyahu will try to turn both into a “we are under attack but we survive” campaign message. The opposition will try to turn them into “the security establishment was asleep” attacks. Meanwhile, Gaza and the West Bank will keep producing friction, Europe will get more irritated, the Gulf states will stay cautious, and the far-right will push harder to look tough before the Oct. 27 election.

1. The Oct. 7 anniversary will become a major political earthquake
Likelihood: high

Since today is Oct. 2, the Oct. 7 anniversary falls right inside this next week. That is huge.

What I expect:

Official commemorations, state events, Knesset or cabinet remarks, and Netanyahu speeches.
Opposition leaders, especially Gadi Eisenkot and others, will hammer the leak that Egypt warned Netanyahu before Oct. 7.
Netanyahu’s camp will probably say: “We were warned? Then we know what to do. We are stronger now.”
The opposition will say: “You knew, you did not act, and now the country is paying for it.”
New polls may be released around this moment, and they will likely try to measure whether the warning leaks hurt Netanyahu.
There may be a demand for an emergency Knesset session or security cabinet meeting.
This is probably the most politically dangerous week for Netanyahu before the election. The plane incident helps him look like a protector, but the Oct. 7 warning leaks attack his core security record. Those two stories will collide.

2. The FlyDubai cockpit attack will become a national obsession
Likelihood: very high

This story will not fade quickly. It landed in Israel with Israelis returning from Dubai, it involved a possible attempt to crash the plane, and it happened weeks before an election. That is perfect political fuel.

What I expect:

Netanyahu will keep calling it a near-disaster, almost a “9/11-like” moment.
The hero Indian pilot, Captain Smit Machchhar, and passenger Yaniv Hayoun will receive more public praise, possibly official awards or ceremonies.
Israel will announce tightened security checks on pilots flying into Ben Gurion and other airports.
Expect new rules or reviews around cockpit access, pilot verification, foreign crews, emergency procedures, and maybe even biometric or identity checks.
Shin Bet, the aviation authority, the military, and possibly the police will be involved in the review.
UAE, Saudi Arabia, Oman, and Israel will keep issuing careful statements about the probe.
The fact that the copilot is reportedly Omani makes Trump’s “if Iran is behind it, we hit them very hard” line awkward. So next week will be a fight over evidence: was this personal? medical? criminal? terrorist? Iranian-linked?
If the investigation points toward terrorism or an Iranian proxy, expect:

Trump to escalate rhetoric.
Israel to raise its security alert level.
More US military deployments or visible reinforcements in the region.
Netanyahu to use it as proof that Israel is under a wider threat.
If the investigation points more toward personal, medical, or criminal motives, expect:

Opposition attacks that Netanyahu overstated it.
But Netanyahu will still use it politically anyway.
Either way, cockpit security becomes a national topic for at least the next week, probably longer.

3. Netanyahu will run a “protector of Israel” campaign
Likelihood: high

The headlines already show him using his old playbook: project strength, survival, and military confidence.

What I expect:

Likud ads and speeches will emphasize: “We survived the plane attack. We are fighting Hamas. We are dealing with Iran. We are protecting Israel.”
Netanyahu will try to make voters feel that choosing him is choosing security.
His far-right coalition partners will push hard to energize their base.
Itamar Ben-Gvir will likely make more provocative moves: Al-Aqsa incursions, West Bank speeches, prayers for “the death of all terrorists,” and other gestures designed to fire up the right.
Bezalel Smotrich will push settlement-friendly or economically populist moves, including fuel price subsidies, to keep his base calm before the election.
The opposition will attack him on:

Oct. 7 warnings.
The cost of the war.
Settlement expansion.
Gaza humanitarian pressure.
The cockpit incident as proof of security failure.
Coalition infighting and far-right provocations.
Netanyahu’s problem is that he can sell fear, but if people feel the security system was asleep, fear can turn against him.

4. The West Bank will get noisier and more violent
Likelihood: high

The headlines show settlers attacking villages, blocking families, seizing water sources, marching between settlements during Sukkot, and Israel’s military sometimes struggling to control the situation.

What I expect:

More settler marches in the northern West Bank, around Jerusalem, Hebron, Nablus, Tulkarm, or other sensitive areas.
More clashes between settlers, Palestinians, and Israeli forces.
More complaints from Palestinian families about blocked roads, burned homes, or prevented returns.
President Herzog will probably condemn settler violence, but the government will likely keep its action limited.
Smotrich and Ben-Gvir will use the West Bank to look tough.
Israel may announce more security closures, checkpoints, or restrictions around Jewish holidays.
European countries, especially the Netherlands, UK, France, and others, will keep criticizing settlement expansion.
This is not likely to become a full new war in the West Bank next week, but it will be a persistent source of bad headlines.

5. Gaza will continue without a full ceasefire
Likelihood: high

The death toll is already over 74,000 according to Gaza’s Health Ministry, and strikes are still happening. The war is now nearly three years old. That means it has become a long grinding conflict rather than a short campaign.

What I expect:

More Israeli strikes in Gaza, especially around areas Israel says Hamas or other groups operate from.
More casualty updates from Gaza’s Health Ministry.
Humanitarian organizations will keep reporting on displacement, the “Yellow Line,” camp conditions, and restricted movement.
Qatar will keep pushing for a ceasefire or humanitarian package.
The US-created Board of Peace and Israel will continue to push ideas about post-war Gaza governance that sideline UNRWA.
Hamas and Palestinian factions will reject or criticize any plan they see as erasing the refugee issue.
Netanyahu’s government will use battlefield claims in its election campaign, but it will also feel pressure from economic costs, hostage issues if still relevant, and international criticism.
A full ceasefire next week is possible but not the most likely outcome. More likely: continued strikes, partial humanitarian talks, and public fighting over who is responsible for the lack of a deal.

6. UAE, Saudi Arabia, Oman, and Iran will keep the Gulf tense but cautious
Likelihood: medium-high

Netanyahu’s secret Abu Dhabi visit is a big story. He reportedly shared new intelligence on Iran’s nuclear program with Sheikh Mohammed bin Zayed. That shows Israel and the UAE are coordinating more closely, even if they do not always announce it publicly.

What I expect:

More leaks or details about the Netanyahu-MBS meeting.
The UAE will try to balance closeness to Israel with caution after the FlyDubai cockpit incident.
Saudi Arabia will stay careful. It already turned down Israel’s request to allow its planes to pick up Israeli passengers, which shows Riyadh does not want to appear too cozy or too exposed.
Oman may become more involved in statements because the copilot is reportedly Omani.
US-Iran back-channel talks may continue through mediators.
Trump will keep threatening Iran if evidence links it to the cockpit attack, but the Omani connection makes that harder to use cleanly.
There may be visible US military movement in the region — ships, aircraft, air defense systems, or additional troops — which will raise Israel’s alert level.
Full normalization with Saudi Arabia is not likely next week. More likely: quiet coordination, public caution, and economic/security signaling.

7. Europe will annoy Israel more, but probably not break it
Likelihood: medium-high

The Netherlands revoked or had revoked Israeli diplomats’ status over settlement sanctions. The UN expanded its list of companies linked to settlements. The UK pledged a tougher stance. Israel is courting Europe’s far right while being criticized by center-left governments.

What I expect:

More diplomatic sniping between Israel and European capitals.
Possible Israeli retaliation: visa restrictions, diplomat accreditation issues, trade complaints, or customs disputes.
More settlement-related boycotts or product-labeling pressure from Europe.
Israeli business groups will complain about economic isolation.
Netanyahu’s government will say Europe is punishing Israel for winning a war.
European far-right leaders may visit or make supportive statements, giving Netanyahu some political cover.
This will not decide the election by itself, but it adds to the feeling that Israel is becoming more diplomatically isolated even as it gets militarily stronger.

8. The economy will feel pressure from politics
Likelihood: medium

The ZIM/Hapag-Lloyd deal being halted because of Saudi and Qatari stakes is a sign that Gulf money and settlement politics are now touching Israel’s economy.

What I expect:

More scrutiny on deals involving Gulf-linked investors or companies with settlement links.
Smotrich may announce or extend fuel price subsidies to keep voters calm before the election.
The shekel and market may wobble around security news, but probably not crash unless there is a major regional escalation.
Tourism and aviation will feel the FlyDubai incident, especially if more airlines suspend or reduce Israel flights.
Business leaders will worry about “economic isolation by sanctions,” even if it is still early.
The economy is not going to dominate the headlines as much as security and elections, but it will be a background pressure on the government.

9. The legal battle over Arab parties and election eligibility will continue
Likelihood: medium-high

The withdrawal of Sami Abu Shehadeh after a Supreme Court indication shows that the courts are becoming part of the election fight.

What I expect:

More petitions, withdrawals, or party-name disputes.
The coalition may try to use legal rulings to weaken Arab parties.
The opposition and Arab parties will attack Israel for narrowing democratic participation.
If a major court ruling comes out, it could change polling math or force last-minute coalition reshuffles.
This issue will not dominate every headline, but it will keep the election messy.

10. Security services will stay on high alert
Likelihood: medium-high

The cockpit attack, the UK air base bomb plot, IRGC claims about killing terrorists with explosives in Iran, and possible US troop movements all point to a region that is still very tense.

What I expect:

More counterterrorism alerts.
Possible arrests or security operations in Israel.
Shin Bet coordination with UAE, Saudi Arabia, Oman, and the US.
Tighter checks at airports, borders, public transport, schools, and major events.
More public debate about cockpit doors, pilot training, foreign crews, and emergency procedures.
A new major terror attack is possible, but not guaranteed. The main thing is that Israel will act like it is possible.

11. Sports and culture will keep adding pressure
Likelihood: medium-low, but visible

The Ireland match with no handshakes and black armbands, plus Hapoel Beer-Sheva being banned from having away fans in two Europa League games, show that Israel is still politically exposed through sport.

What I expect:

More debates about boycotts, antisemitism, and whether playing against Israel is a political statement.
Israeli teams abroad may face more hostile atmospheres.
Domestic Israeli media will use these moments to argue both sides: “They are boycotting us because of Gaza” and “We are being punished for the war.”
This will not decide the election, but it adds to the international mood around Israel.

My best single bet for the next week
If I had to summarize the next seven days in one sentence:

Israel will spend the week managing two security shocks — the Oct. 7 warning leaks and the FlyDubai cockpit attack — while Netanyahu tries to turn fear into votes, the far-right pushes West Bank and Gaza hard, Europe gets more irritated, the Gulf stays cautious, and no full ceasefire appears unless a surprise deal emerges.

What I think will dominate day by day
First part of the week:

FlyDubai aftermath.
Pilot security checks.
Hero pilot/passenger stories.
UAE, Saudi, Oman, and Israeli statements.
Netanyahu using the incident to boost his security image.
Around Oct. 7:

Anniversary commemorations.
Netanyahu speech or major campaign event.
Opposition rallies over the warning leaks.
New polls.
Possible cabinet or Knesset debate.
Middle and late part of the week:

West Bank settler marches and clashes.
Gaza strike updates.
European diplomatic criticism.
Election campaign ads intensifying.
Possible fuel/subsidy announcements from Smotrich.
More legal fights over Arab party eligibility.
What I would not bet on for next week
I would not bet heavily on:

A full Gaza ceasefire being signed next week.
A formal war with Iran unless the cockpit attack is clearly linked to Iran or a major new strike happens.
The government collapsing before the election.
A Supreme Court ruling immediately disqualifying a major party, though smaller rulings are possible.
Saudi normalization becoming official next week.
The economy crashing because of settlement boycotts, though pressure will grow.
Bottom line
Next week in Israel is going to feel like a pressure cooker.

Netanyahu’s advantage is that the plane incident lets him say: “Look how dangerous the world is, and look how we survived.”
His danger is that the Oct. 7 warning leaks let the opposition say: “You were warned, and you still failed us.”

The far-right will push West Bank moves because it helps their base.
Gaza will keep producing casualty headlines.
Europe will keep applying diplomatic pressure.
The Gulf will stay useful but cautious.
And the cockpit attack will turn into a national argument about security, trust, foreign pilots, and whether Israel’s institutions are really protecting the country.

So my blunt prediction: more fear, more election fighting, more West Bank friction, more Gaza strikes, more cockpit-security talk, and no calm.
