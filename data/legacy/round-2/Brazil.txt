Oi :-) I read all of it. Assuming “next week” means the seven days after the Oct 4 first round — roughly Oct 7–13, before the Oct 25 runoff — here is my straight prediction for Brazil.

Short version
Brazil will spend the next week in a high-voltage, polarized sprint toward the Oct 25 runoff. Flávio Bolsonaro will likely remain the favorite, but not as safely as markets initially implied. Lula will try to pivot toward the center and sound more “market-friendly,” especially by testing the idea of putting Geraldo Alckmin in charge of the economy if he wins. The right will push harder on fiscal austerity, security, and anti-establishment themes. Markets will probably stay bullish but nervous. Streets will get louder. The TSE will likely finalize/certify first-round results during that week or just after it, clearing the way for full-blown runoff campaigning.

No major constitutional crisis is likely in the next seven days, but the tension around the Supreme Court, embassies, and election institutions will remain visible.

High-confidence predictions: what is very likely to happen
1. The TSE will move toward certifying the first-round results
The most important institutional event of the week will be the electoral court’s process for officially confirming the first-round outcome. In recent Brazilian elections, that certification happens days after voting and before the second round fully ramps up.

So expect:

Flávio Bolsonaro’s first-place finish to be formally confirmed.
Lula’s second place to be confirmed.
Minor legal challenges or procedural complaints from smaller candidates or parties.
A high likelihood that no serious delay occurs.
The runoff campaign becoming more official and intense after certification.
This does not mean the race is over. It means the two-candidate phase becomes the main political battlefield.

2. Lula will try to “shake up” his campaign with a centrist economic signal
The Reuters item about Lula weighing Vice President Geraldo Alckmin as next finance minister is probably the most important clue for the next week. That is not just personnel talk. It is a message to the center, the markets, and undecided voters:

“Do not fear a radical leftist government.”
“We can govern with fiscal responsibility.”
“The market does not have to choose between Bolsonaro and chaos.”
So in the next week, Lula’s camp will likely:

Test the Alckmin-as-finance-minister idea more openly.
Speak directly to investors, business leaders, banks, and financial media.
Try to present a “market Lula” version of his campaign.
Emphasize continuity, stability, and institutional credibility.
Court centrist voters who are uncomfortable with Flávio Bolsonaro’s austerity plans.
This is Lula’s best chance to prevent the race from becoming a simple left-versus-right referendum. If he makes the Alckmin signal credible, it could narrow the gap in some polls. If it feels too late or too transactional, it may not move enough voters.

3. Flávio Bolsonaro will push harder on austerity and fiscal “scissors”
The Financial Times piece about Flávio Bolsonaro’s plan to “wield the big scissors” on Brazil’s budget is a strong hint that his campaign will now lean more into market-friendly reform language.

Expect him to:

Talk more about spending cuts.
Promise fiscal discipline.
Appeal to investors and business voters.
Position himself as a modernized Bolsonaro: still right-wing, but less chaotic than his father’s image.
Try to sound like a serious future president rather than just the heir to Jair Bolsonaro’s movement.
He will probably not go full Milei-style chainsaw rhetoric in public, because Brazil is bigger and more institutionally complex than Argentina. But he will definitely use the “big scissors” theme: spending cuts, tax reform talk, privatization hints, and a promise to make the state smaller or at least cheaper.

4. Markets will probably stay bullish but volatile
The headlines show that Brazilian assets already surged after the first round:

Ibovespa near record levels.
Real stronger.
Bonds moving.
Record foreign inflows into Brazilian stocks.
Wall Street loading up on options.
Banks upgrading Brazilian equities.
Petrobras being framed as an election-beneficiary trade.
So in the next week, I expect:

Continued foreign interest in Brazilian assets.
More upgrades and bullish commentary from international banks.
A stronger-than-usual inflow into Brazilian stocks.
Petrobras likely to remain a favorite trade if privatization/energy reform expectations stay alive.
But also more nervousness around long-term bonds if investors worry that Flávio Bolsonaro’s fiscal plan is too ambitious or politically difficult.
The market’s base case is still: Flávio Bolsonaro wins, markets continue to rally.

But the market will be watching polls very closely. If Lula closes the gap suddenly, expect a sharp dip in Brazilian assets and a spike in options activity. If Flávio looks safe, expect continued strength.

5. Polls will show a lead for Bolsonaro, but not a comfortable one
The headlines are mixed on how strong Flávio’s lead is. Some sources say “commanding,” others say “nail-biter,” and AP is already emphasizing undecided and abstaining voters. That suggests the next week will be full of polling noise.

My prediction:

Most new polls will still show Flávio Bolsonaro ahead.
But the gap will probably not look as large as the first-round result initially suggested.
Lula may gain some traction among centrist, female, urban middle-class, and welfare-dependent voters who are scared by both Bolsonaro-style austerity and Lula’s age/political fatigue.
The race will remain close enough that neither side can afford a major mistake.
In plain terms: Flávio is likely still the favorite, but not yet the safe winner.

6. Street protests will intensify, especially on the left
The AFP and DW items about student marches and “Never again Bolsonaro” protests are important. The first-round result clearly energized the left and anti-Bolsonaro street movement.

So in the next week, expect:

More student-led marches in Rio, São Paulo, Belo Horizonte, Brasília, and other university cities.
Larger rallies by Lula’s base in the Northeast and in major metropolitan areas.
Counter-protests from Bolsonaro supporters, especially around São Paulo, Brasília, and conservative strongholds.
More visible polarization in public spaces.
A higher chance of small clashes near embassies, government buildings, or Supreme Court/TSE areas.
The protests will probably remain mostly peaceful, but they will be loud and symbolic. The left will use the streets to create fear among undecided voters: “Bolsonaro is coming.” The right will use its own rallies to show strength and demand change.

7. Security around key institutions will stay elevated
The mention of a bomb scare outside the Supreme Court, plus the US and Australia suspending consular services before the election, means security will remain tight.

Expect:

Heavier police presence around the STF, TSE, government buildings, and foreign missions.
More scrutiny of protests near sensitive locations.
Possible small demonstrations outside US or Australian embassies/consulates.
Continued diplomatic irritation between Brasília and Washington over election interference claims.
A high chance that consular services resume after the first round unless a new security threat appears.
This does not mean Brazil is in crisis. It means the country’s institutions are under political stress, and both sides know that courts, embassies, and electoral authorities will become battlegrounds.

Medium-confidence predictions: likely, but not certain
1. The right-heavy Congress will start positioning for a post-Oct 25 government
The headlines about sweeping right-wing gains in Congress are important. Even before the runoff, the new congressional composition is already shaping expectations.

In the next week, expect:

Right-wing leaders to start talking about institutional control.
More pressure to discuss amnesty or legal relief for Jair Bolsonaro.
Early lobbying over future appointments to powerful institutions.
Environmental and indigenous groups warning that a right-heavy Congress could weaken protections.
Business groups trying to position themselves ahead of possible privatization and tax reform.
A formal vote on Jair Bolsonaro’s amnesty probably will not happen in the next week, but the political conversation will definitely intensify. The Bolsonaro brand is already winning without being on the ballot, and the right will try to convert that into institutional power.

2. Lula may try to turn Alckmin into a full campaign message
The Alckmin-as-finance-minister idea could become more than a rumor. If Lula’s team believes it can calm markets and pull centrists, they may push it harder during the next week.

Possible moves:

A public statement from Lula or his campaign about economic continuity.
Meetings between Lula’s team and Alckmin allies.
Financial media coverage trying to test whether this is real.
Market reaction if the idea sounds credible.
Criticism from Lula’s left base, which may see it as selling out.
This is one of the biggest swing factors in the next week. If “Alckmin as finance minister” becomes a believable Lula promise, it could help him narrow the gap. If it looks like desperation, it may not help much.

3. Flávio Bolsonaro will try to broaden his coalition beyond the Bolsonaro base
Flávio cannot win only with Jair’s old core. He needs centrists, business voters, security-focused voters, and enough of the undecided middle.

So expect him to:

Sound more presidential.
Emphasize competence, not just Bolsonaro loyalty.
Talk about public safety, corruption, and fiscal reform.
Try to reassure investors without sounding like a technocrat.
Use Trump’s support as a strength, while also trying not to look too dependent on Washington.
His challenge is that the Bolsonaro brand is powerful but also polarizing. He needs to be Bolsonaro enough for the base, but not Bolsonaro enough to scare the center.

4. The Amazon and climate will become campaign issues again
The AP item about the future of Amazon rainforest policies is relevant. Environmental groups are already worried about right-wing gains in Congress.

In the next week:

Lula’s camp will likely use climate, Amazon protection, and global leadership as campaign themes.
Flávio’s camp will probably try to sound pragmatic: development plus sustainability, but less “green tax” language.
International environmental groups may issue more warnings.
Lula may court global left and climate voters to keep his base energized.
This is unlikely to decide the election by itself, but it will matter for Lula’s identity as a global leader and for his support among urban, educated, and international-minded voters.

5. The US factor will stay very visible
Trump’s support for Flávio Bolsonaro is already part of the story. Brazil accusing the US of election interference, plus the consular suspension, means Washington-Brasília friction will remain in the background.

Expect:

More Trump-friendly statements from Flávio’s camp.
More criticism from Lula’s camp about US interference.
Diplomatic spats, but probably no full crisis.
Continued media focus on whether Brazil is becoming more aligned with Washington under Bolsonaro or more confrontational under Lula.
Some Brazilian nationalists using the consular suspension as proof of foreign meddling.
The US will not directly decide the runoff, but it will be a major campaign theme.

Lower-probability events that could still matter
These are less likely in just one week, but they would change the story fast if they happen.

1. A surprise poll puts the race within the margin of error
If new polling shows Lula and Flávio statistically tied, expect:

A sharp market wobble.
More intense options trading.
Sudden media panic on both sides.
Lula’s camp claiming a comeback.
Flávio’s camp stressing that first-round momentum matters.
Both campaigns trying to lock in undecided voters before the next wave of polls.
This is not impossible. The AP headline about undecided and abstaining voters suggests the race is not as settled as it may look.

2. A security incident around the STF, TSE, or embassies
Given the bomb scare and consular suspensions, a new security episode could happen. It does not need to be huge to matter. Even a small incident can feed polarization.

Possible effects:

More protests outside courts and embassies.
Increased police presence.
Accusations of political violence from both sides.
A short spike in media drama.
Possibly more pressure on the TSE or STF to move quickly and visibly.
3. Flávio’s austerity plan triggers a fiscal scare
If his “big scissors” message sounds too radical, investors may worry about:

Public debt.
Spending cuts that hurt growth.
Political resistance in Congress.
Social backlash from welfare-dependent voters.
A possible bond-market sell-off.
That would not kill his campaign, but it could make his lead less comfortable and give Lula a stronger argument: “Bolsonaro will cut services you depend on.”

4. Lula has a visible health or energy problem
Lula’s age is already part of the narrative. The headlines mention him running on a treadmill to show energy. That means any stumble, cough, pause, or unusual appearance could be amplified.

If that happens:

Media will focus heavily on it.
Flávio’s camp will attack his capacity to govern.
Lula’s base may rally around him as a symbol.
The race could become more emotional and less policy-focused.
This is not the most likely event, but in a Brazilian election, images matter a lot.

5. A minor legal challenge delays TSE certification by a few days
A full delay is unlikely, but a short procedural delay is possible. If the TSE does not certify on time, both campaigns will still operate as if the runoff is already the main event. The political effect would be limited, but it could create more anxiety.

What I would watch most closely in the next seven days
If you want to know which way Brazil is moving, watch these things:

TSE certification timing
If the first-round results are officially confirmed smoothly, the runoff campaign becomes fully official and intense.
New polling averages
The key question is not whether Flávio leads, but how much he leads. A 3-point lead is very different from a 10-point lead.
Lula’s Alckmin signal
If Lula seriously pushes the idea of Alckmin as finance minister, that is his best centrist rescue move. Watch whether markets react positively and whether undecided voters seem to respond.
Flávio’s fiscal plan
If he gives concrete details on spending cuts, tax reform, or privatization, that will tell you whether he is trying to win the center or just the Bolsonaro base.
Street protest size
If left protests grow significantly in Rio, São Paulo, and Belo Horizonte, that may help Lula among undecided voters. If right counter-protests are large too, it shows the country remains deeply split.
Brazilian market behavior
Watch the Ibovespa, real, bond yields, and Petrobras. If markets keep rallying, they are pricing a Flávio win. If there is a sudden dip, something has shaken investor confidence.
US-Brazil diplomatic tone
More consular suspensions or stronger accusations of interference would add tension. A quiet resumption of services would mean the immediate security fear is fading.
My bottom-line prediction for Brazil in the next week
Brazil will not have a full crisis in the next seven days, but it will become more intense and more polarized. The most likely path is:

Flávio Bolsonaro remains the favorite.
Lula tries to pivot center with Alckmin and market-friendly language.
The right pushes austerity and institutional control.
Markets stay broadly bullish but sensitive to polls.
Protests grow, especially on the left.
The TSE moves toward certifying first-round results.
The runoff campaign becomes the central focus of Brazilian public life.
If I had to bet:

Flávio Bolsonaro is still likely to win the Oct 25 runoff unless Lula’s centrist shake-up and street pressure create a late surge among undecided voters.

The next week will not decide the election, but it will set the emotional tone for the final two weeks. Brazil will spend those days trying to answer one question:

Is this a Bolsonaro comeback that markets love and streets fear, or is Lula still able to hold the center together long enough to survive?
