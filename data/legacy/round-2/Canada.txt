Assuming “next week” starts around the latest headlines, roughly Oct 7–14, 2026, here is my unfiltered forecast for Canada. No tables, as requested.

Short version: Canada’s next week will be dominated by a Quebec sovereignty shock, an Alberta unity referendum looming just outside or at the edge of the window, continued US trade pressure, a weak loonie, and Carney using infrastructure — especially the Pacific oil pipeline — as his main political weapon to keep the country together.

The country is not going to break apart in seven days. But it will feel much more politically tense than usual, because Ottawa is being tested from two directions at once: Quebec on one side, Alberta on the other, with a belligerent Trump administration in the background.

Most likely sequence of events over the next week:

Quebec separatists form a minority government within days
This is the biggest immediate political event. The Parti Québécois has won power but not a majority, so expect coalition or confidence-and-supply negotiations to dominate the first 48–72 hours. Paul St-Pierre Plamondon will almost certainly become premier, and the province’s new government will immediately start positioning itself around an independence referendum promise.
Likely outcome:

PQ forms government with support from at least one other party or caucus.
No immediate official referendum date is set, but the language of “Quebec deserves to be a country” becomes the central narrative.
Ottawa will try to avoid making this look like a constitutional emergency in the first week.
Confidence: high.

Plamondon’s refusal to swear allegiance to King Charles III becomes a national story
This is one of the more interesting details in your headlines. If the new Quebec leader says he will not swear allegiance to the king, that will create a symbolic constitutional flashpoint, even if it does not immediately block him from governing.
What I expect:

Federal officials will publicly downplay the issue privately while debating it internally.
Legal and constitutional experts will flood the media with explanations about whether there is a workaround.
The most likely result is some kind of modified oath, delayed swearing-in, or political compromise rather than a full constitutional crisis.
Carney will probably avoid making this a major public fight in the first week because he does not want to turn Quebec’s election into an immediate monarchy showdown.
Confidence: high that it becomes a big story; medium that it is resolved quickly without drama.

Carney makes a national unity response, but not a dramatic constitutional one
Carney has already said Quebec should focus on the economy rather than wait for a possible independence referendum years away. That tells you how he will frame this: not as an emotional fight over symbols, but as a practical argument that Quebec is better off in Canada if Ottawa can deliver prosperity, housing, energy exports, and reduced dependence on the US.
Likely next-week actions:

A statement from Carney or his office reinforcing that unity depends on economic action.
A cabinet meeting focused on Quebec, Alberta, trade, and national cohesion.
Possibly a phone call or brief meeting between Carney and Plamondon.
A push to announce or promise an economic package for Quebec — housing, infrastructure, fiscal transfers, energy, or workforce investment.
What I do not expect in the first week:

A sudden federal constitutional proposal.
An immediate national referendum process.
Carney publicly threatening Quebec.
A full-blown crisis over the Crown unless Plamondon’s oath issue gets more extreme than expected.
Confidence: high for a measured unity response; medium for an economic package being announced or strongly promised within the week.

Alberta’s separation vote becomes the second major stress test
The Alberta referendum is probably the biggest looming event in your list, especially because Carney fast-tracked the Pacific oil pipeline right before it. The political message is obvious: Ottawa is trying to keep Alberta in Canada by giving it a concrete economic reason — faster energy exports, jobs, and reduced dependence on the US market.
If your “next week” includes Oct 19:

I would expect a narrow No victory, not a landslide.
Turnout will matter a lot.
The result will be framed nationally as either relief for Carney or another warning sign about western separatism.
If the vote falls just outside the seven-day window:

The final campaign will dominate late in the week.
Polls will likely show No ahead, but not by enough to make everyone comfortable.
Ottawa will be watching every exit poll and early-voting signal with intense anxiety.
My read on the outcome:

No is more likely than Yes because separation is expensive and uncertain, and the pipeline fast-track gives the federal government a tangible “stay in Canada” argument.
But the margin could be close enough to keep Carney awake at night.
If Yes wins, that would be a major national shock and would force Ottawa into a much more defensive posture.
Confidence: medium-high for No; medium for narrow margin; low for a dramatic Yes surprise.

The Pacific oil pipeline becomes the centerpiece of Carney’s “build Canada” agenda
This is not just an energy story. It is a political test for Carney. If he can fast-track a major project, it supports his narrative that Canada can build faster, diversify away from the US, and give provinces like Alberta a reason to stay in the federation.
Likely next-week developments:

More details on the single federal regulatory review process.
Government statements about timelines, environmental assessment, labour plans, and supply-chain benefits.
Pushback from unions, environmental groups, Indigenous communities, or legal critics over consultation and speed.
Energy and construction stocks getting attention in markets.
This pipeline is also tied directly to the Alberta referendum. Carney is using it as a unity tool: “If we can build this fast and give you export options, why leave?”

Confidence: high that this becomes one of the main policy stories of the week.

US-Canada trade pressure continues, with more rhetoric and possibly targeted retaliation
The headlines show a clear pattern: Trump is treating Canada as a trade adversary, Canadian officials are pushing back, and the loonie is weak. The US ban on some Canadian imports — alcohol, dairy, motorcycles — is already creating consumer and retail effects, and G20 talks failed to produce a global fix on overproduction.
What I expect next week:

More cabinet-level meetings on trade retaliation and negotiation strategy.
Canadian officials talking about “targeted” responses rather than a full trade war escalation.
Possible hints of countermeasures against US products — bourbon, autos, farm goods, or other politically sensitive items.
Doug Ford continuing to feed the feud with Trump because it is useful for Ontario politics.
Ottawa emphasizing diversification toward Europe, Asia, and other partners.
I would not expect a full trade deal in one week. I would expect more posturing, pressure, and small escalations.

Confidence: high that US-Canada trade friction stays front and center; medium that Canada announces or strongly hints at new retaliatory measures within the week.

The loonie stays weak, near 18-month lows
The Canadian dollar is already near an 18-month low against the US dollar. A wider trade surplus helped it briefly, but the bigger forces are still negative: strong US dollar, US trade pressure, global risk sentiment, and oil price uncertainty.
Likely next-week range:

The loonie probably stays in a weak band around US$0.70–0.72 unless there is a major oil move or macro surprise.
A stronger trade surplus may prevent a sharp crash, but it is unlikely to reverse the trend quickly.
If oil falls because of G7 diesel releases or Middle East risk easing, that adds downside pressure on the loonie.
If oil spikes because of Middle East conflict, that gives the loonie temporary support.
Confidence: high that the loonie remains weak and volatile.

The Bank of Canada will not solve housing affordability by itself
The central bank has already said Canada is not close to restoring housing affordability, and its second-in-command says rates are not the right tool for fixing house prices. That sets up a political problem for Carney: his “build” agenda needs workers, construction, and supply-side action, but inflation expectations are rising and the Bank may still be weighing hikes rather than cuts.
What I expect:

If there is a Bank of Canada policy meeting in the window, no dramatic surprise move.
More likely a hold with cautious language, or a small hike if inflation data looks sticky.
Officials continuing to say rates cannot fix housing alone.
Political pressure on Carney to address supply, foreign buyer rules, zoning, immigration-driven demand, and construction speed.
This will not be the loudest story, but it matters because housing is one of the main domestic issues that could undermine Carney’s popularity if ignored.

Confidence: medium-high that the Bank stays cautious; high that housing affordability remains a political sore point.

More media focus on cross-border crime, online coordination, and whether Canada’s legal tools are adequate.

Confidence: high that extradition/process news continues; medium for a formal public-safety response being announced.

Carney’s “US attack scenarios” comment keeps defence debate alive
