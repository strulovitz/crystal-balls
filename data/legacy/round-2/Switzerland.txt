Assuming “next week” means roughly the next 7 days from around Oct 8–15, 2026, here is my unfiltered read. No tables, as requested.

The short version: Switzerland will not have a domestic crisis in the next week, but it will be very loud internationally because its role as Europe’s financial, legal, diplomatic, and sports-law hub is being tested at the same time. The main storylines will collide: Parmelin’s resignation, UBS/capital-rule pressure, strong-franc safe-haven flows, glacier/drought alarm, CAS/FIFA drama in Lausanne and Zurich, and Geneva diplomacy.

My overall prediction is that Switzerland will look like a country where money, law, sport, climate, and foreign policy are all arguing over the same reputation: stability, neutrality, wealth, precision, and alpine image.

Most likely things to happen next week

Guy Parmelin’s resignation will keep dominating Swiss political coverage Confidence: very high, maybe 85–95%
This is probably the biggest purely domestic Swiss story. Parmelin has announced he will leave the Federal Council at the end of the year, and he has just been on a high-profile visit to India with Modi, signing MoUs on mobility, transport, science, and innovation. That means two things will happen at once:

He will still be acting as Swiss president/federal council member in the short term, so foreign and economic diplomacy will continue under his name.
The political class will immediately start thinking about who replaces him, because the Federal Council election is likely to become a major December story.
Next week I would expect:

More media interviews or statements from Parmelin about his reasons for leaving.
Follow-up coverage of the India visit and the signed MoUs.
Swiss business press pushing the “Switzerland–India economic ties” angle.
Party-level speculation, especially from the SVP, about a possible successor.
Discussion about how his departure affects the 2027 Federal Presidency rotation, because he is leaving at the end of his one-year term as president.
I would not expect a formal replacement candidate to be confirmed next week unless a party leaks one early. Swiss politics tends to move slowly in public and fast behind closed doors. But the “who replaces Parmelin?” story will be everywhere.

UBS will remain the center of Switzerland’s financial drama Confidence: very high, maybe 85–90%
The UBS headlines are extremely important. The theme is clear: investors are angry about Swiss capital rules, some are calling for UBS to leave Switzerland, and the bank is being pushed between staying in Switzerland and optimizing its global structure. CEO Sergio Ermotti’s message that UBS needs a US presence to succeed fits perfectly into this fight.

My prediction: UBS will not actually leave Switzerland next week. That would be too big, too fast, and too politically explosive. Instead, the next week will look more like “stay for now, negotiate harder later.”

I expect:

More CEO/board/investor commentary on capital rules.
Shareholder pressure to make Switzerland more bank-friendly or at least give UBS more flexibility.
Possible formal responses from UBS, FINMA, or parliamentary circles about the capital fight.
Continued debate over whether Swiss banks are being squeezed by regulation while European competitors get different treatment.
Volatility in Swiss bank stocks, especially if French debt stress or European banking worries flare up again.
The phrase “stay-and-pay” seems like the base case. UBS stays Swiss-domiciled for now, but keeps using the threat of relocation as leverage. The deeper story is that Switzerland may be losing its comfortable position as the home base for a global champion bank unless it becomes more flexible on regulation, taxation, and international competitiveness.

Related financial stories will also keep appearing:

Julius Baer’s share buyback will support confidence in Swiss private banking.
Partners Group restructuring its flagship fund shows that even wealthy Swiss asset managers are under client pressure for liquidity.
The “billionaires leaving the UK for Switzerland, Monaco, and UAE” story will feed into debates about wealth relocation, tax planning, and whether Switzerland is becoming an even more important haven for global capital.
The Swiss franc will remain a major market story Confidence: high, maybe 75–85%
The franc has been rallying because of French budget worries, European bond stress, weak US jobs data, and safe-haven demand. At the same time, there is some back-and-forth because rate expectations outside Switzerland can also move the currency. So the next week will probably be volatile, but directionally the franc remains strong unless global risk appetite suddenly improves.

I would expect:

The franc to stay bid against the euro if France’s debt/budget problems worsen.
Possible intraday slides if US tech stocks rally hard or global risk assets recover.
More commentary from exporters, airlines, tourism companies, and industry groups about how a strong franc hurts them.
The Swiss National Bank to remain on watch mode. If the franc overdoes it, SNB may hint at willingness to act or actually intervene, but I would not bet on a major surprise rate decision unless energy inflation or external shocks force their hand.
The key point: Switzerland’s currency is being treated as part of the European safe-haven complex. That is good for stability, bad for exporters and tourism, and politically inconvenient because it keeps bringing exchange-rate pressure into domestic debates.

The CAS case in Lausanne will probably be the biggest international sports-law story Confidence: high, maybe 75–85% that it dominates news; lower confidence on exact outcome
The Senegal–Morocco Africa Cup of Nations title appeal at the Court of Arbitration for Sport is a huge one. This is not just football gossip. It is a test of Swiss sports-law credibility at the highest level, with global audiences, national pride, money, and FIFA/CAF politics involved.

My prediction: the next week will produce either a ruling or a very clear procedural outcome from Lausanne. If the panel does not deliver an immediate award, expect a statement that still causes massive football-media fallout.

On the actual result, I would be careful. My lean is that CAS will try to avoid a headline that looks like it simply handed the trophy to one side without acknowledging the chaos of the original final. The most likely outcomes are:

Morocco’s default victory is upheld, but with procedural criticism or conditions that leave Senegal angry.
Senegal gets some relief, possibly through a replay, partial award, or remand that forces CAF/FIFA to deal with the issue again.
A technical ruling that satisfies neither side fully and keeps the saga alive.
Either way, expect:

Global football media storms.
Strong reactions from Senegalese and Moroccan fans, federations, and politicians.
CAF and FIFA statements or follow-up questions.
Swiss sports-law experts commenting on credibility, consistency, and the future of CAS.
More noise around whether the title should be shared, replayed, or confirmed.
This story will make Switzerland look like the legal capital of world football again, for better or worse.

Switzerland is directly involved because FIFA headquarters is based in the Zurich/Nyon area, so this is not just European football politics; it is Swiss institutional news too.

Next week I expect:

More leaks about possible UEFA-backed challengers.
Pressure on UEFA president Aleksander Ceferin to show whether he will run or help build a candidate.
Discussion of names from European football leadership, possibly including senior UEFA officials or respected neutral candidates.
Possible acknowledgment or scheduling update from FIFA’s Ethics Committee regarding Norway’s complaints.
Media speculation about how the 2027 FIFA election could affect Switzerland’s relationship with global football governance.
My read: Infantino is still strong, but the challenge is becoming more organized. The next week will be less about a formal announcement and more about positioning, leaks, and strategy.

The Crans-Montana skiing scandal will keep generating Swiss sports-governance news Confidence: medium-high, maybe 65–75%
The suspension of the organizing president of the Alpine skiing world championships in Crans-Montana while he awaits trial for fraud is a very Swiss story: high-profile sport, elite governance, reputation risk, and legal process. This will not disappear quickly.

Next week I would expect:

A statement or clarification from the organizing committee.
Possible appointment of an interim chair or confirmation that the suspended president remains formally in place but limited in powers.
Sponsor, federation, and tourism-industry reactions.
Media questions about governance, financial controls, and whether the event’s reputation is damaged.
Quiet political attention because major ski events are important for alpine tourism and local economies.
This will not be as globally loud as CAS or FIFA, but it will keep Swiss sports news busy.

Glacier loss and drought imagery will turn into a climate/tourism debate Confidence: high, maybe 75–85%
The headlines about Swiss glaciers losing nearly 20% of their mass in five years, the giant “HELP” sign on a drying lakebed, and Lake Constance ferry suspensions due to low water levels are part of the same story: climate stress is becoming visible in Switzerland’s most iconic landscapes.

Next week I expect:

More follow-up coverage of the glacier report.
Commentary from alpine tourism operators, insurers, transport companies, and local governments.
Political pressure for adaptation funding, water management, and infrastructure investment.
Possible cross-border discussion about Lake Constance low-water operations with Germany and Austria.
More visual media: drying lakes, exposed lakebeds, glacier retreat, and climate activists using the images.
This is not likely to produce an immediate major policy law next week, but it will feed into a longer debate about how Switzerland manages its alpine economy under climate change. The economic stakes are real: tourism, hydropower, transport, insurance, and mountain infrastructure all depend on water and ice.

Next week I expect:

Follow-up consultations or side meetings related to Sudan.
Humanitarian organizations issuing updates from Geneva.
Swiss foreign ministry statements or quiet diplomacy.
Possible broader multilateral activity involving trade, health, climate, or humanitarian institutions based in Geneva.
This will not dominate domestic Swiss politics, but it reinforces Switzerland’s role as a neutral diplomatic hub. It also gives the government a chance to show that its international position still matters even while UBS, banks, and sports law are consuming headlines.

Wealth relocation and private-markets stress will keep appearing in finance coverage Confidence: medium, maybe 50–65%
The billionaire relocation story and the Partners Group fund split point to a broader theme: global wealth is moving, clients are nervous, and Swiss financial institutions are trying to prove they remain stable and attractive.

Discussion of whether Swiss financial centers can keep attracting global capital if regulation becomes more burdensome.
This is not a street-level domestic story, but it matters for Zurich, Geneva, and the Swiss economy.

Bottom line

My top three predictions for Switzerland next week are:

The CAS ruling or procedural outcome in Lausanne will be the loudest international story involving Switzerland, and it will likely leave at least one side angry.
UBS will keep fighting over capital rules and domicile, but it will not actually leave Switzerland next week. “Stay and negotiate” is the base case.
