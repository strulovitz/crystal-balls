MY BOTTOM-LINE CALL

Japan next week will probably look stable on the surface but politically and financially tense underneath. The main domestic story will be the Okinawa US Marine murder case, because it touches base politics, US-Japan relations, local anger, and the government’s credibility. The main financial story will be Takaichi’s spending/growth plan versus Japanese government bond yields and Bank of Japan normalization. What I expect next week:

More protests near US bases, especially around Naha/Kadena areas.
Local Okinawa politicians pressing the central government to do more than just express condolences or demand cooperation.
The Japanese government likely calling for stricter US military discipline, better information sharing, and possibly temporary operational restrictions around sensitive areas.
A visible US-Japan diplomatic effort to manage the case without letting it become a broader base-policy fight.
If prosecutors move quickly, there could be an indictment request, a custody issue, or a demand by the US military for access to the suspect. That would likely intensify protests.
What is less likely in 7 days:

A full renegotiation of the Status of Forces Agreement.
A sudden base withdrawal.
A major formal change in Japan-US security cooperation.
The most likely outcome is: public anger stays high, the government pushes for visible US military discipline, and the case becomes a recurring political issue rather than a one-day shock.

Takaichi spending plan and bond-market nerves dominate financial headlines
Likelihood: high, maybe 75-85%.

The PM has already said she is prepared to review spending and revenue plans if yields move sharply. Finance officials are trying to reassure markets that the policy is not simply “reflationary” or fiscally loose.

What I expect next week:

More detailed communication from the government about the first five years of the large investment plan.
Official reassurance from PM Takaichi, Finance Minister Katayama, and possibly other cabinet members.
Continued volatility in long-end Japanese government bonds, especially the 30-year JGB.
Market debate over whether the Bank of Japan should normalize faster or wait for clearer data.
Possible yen moves if bond yields spike and carry-trade positioning changes.
The key question next week is not “Will the BOJ hike immediately?” The more likely story is: “Can the government explain its spending plan without spooking bond markets?”

If the government releases a clear funding framework, long-end yields may stabilize. If it sounds vague or too expansionary, 30-year JGB yields could jump and force more defensive messaging from Tokyo.

What I expect next week:

Continued document reviews and interviews by the FTC.
Statements from Asahi, Kirin, Suntory, and Sapporo saying they will cooperate.
Possible internal review announcements or hints that some companies found issues in their own records.
Market reaction if any company appears more exposed than others.
Political discussion about whether beer prices have been artificially high and whether consumers should get relief.
A full formal finding may take longer, but the next week will likely be about expanding the investigation and managing public/company statements.

More clarity on criteria, timing, and affected names.
Passive fund rebalancing pressure on some stocks.
Companies announcing governance improvements, share buybacks, consolidations, or other measures to avoid removal.
Some sharp stock moves among borderline companies.
Broader discussion about whether Japanese corporate governance is finally being forced to improve.
This may not feel as emotional as Okinawa, but it could be one of the bigger market-moving stories in Tokyo over the next few days.

Likelihood: high, maybe 70-80%.
Japan raising the permanent residency fee by 20 times is a big policy change, especially because it comes amid labor shortages, aging population, and growing foreign resident numbers. What I expect next week:

Immigration offices issuing guidance or Q&A materials.
Complaints from foreign residents, businesses, and legal advisors about cost and process.
Debate over whether Japan is being too strict on residency while still needing foreign labor.
Possible government clarification on income requirements, pension contributions, and application procedures.
Some applicants delaying applications until rules are better understood.
This will not be a crisis, but it will be a persistent administrative and social-policy story.

City officials holding press conferences or issuing explanations.

A formal audit or review by prefectural/national authorities.
