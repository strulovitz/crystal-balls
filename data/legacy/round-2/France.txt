Here is my blunt prediction for France in the next 7 days, based on those headlines and without tables.

The core story is not just “students protesting schools.” It is a double crisis: street anger plus bond-market fear. The government is being squeezed from below by students, parents, unions, mayors, and local politics, and from above by investors who are punishing French debt because they doubt Paris can fix the deficit without breaking something politically.

Most likely next week in France:

Student protests continue, but become more uneven and tactical
The biggest national demonstrations have probably already happened, so next week will look less like one giant single day and more like repeated local actions: school blockades, marches in Paris, Lille, Nantes, Lyon, Marseille, suburbs, and university towns. Some days will be big, some days smaller. The movement is too broad now to disappear quickly, but it also has not fully become a general strike yet.
Expect:

Hundreds of schools open and closed depending on local pressure.
More targeted suspensions of classes than a total national shutdown.
Continued clashes in sensitive suburban areas.
More arrests, more injuries, more police scrutiny.
Parents and teachers joining students more often, especially where schools are already weak.
The government will try to split the movement by giving visible concessions: extra teachers, emergency funding, repairs, temporary staff, better security around schools, or promises about exam conditions. But because the budget deficit is the bigger problem, those concessions will be politically useful but fiscally limited.

The budget fight becomes the real center of gravity
The student protests are the emotional story, but the bond market is the political boss. The government will probably try to pass its fiscal plan by force or through a very narrow parliamentary deal. Finance Minister Roland Lescure has already signaled willingness to bypass Parliament if needed, so next week could see a 49.3-style procedure or equivalent emergency budget maneuvering.
Likely scenarios:

The government passes the budget with last-minute compromises.
The budget is passed but remains politically fragile.
A no-confidence attempt appears, especially from the left, possibly joined by parts of the right if they can frame it as “Macron’s fiscal shock.”
If the budget fails or barely survives, pressure rises for a political reset: snap election talk, coalition reshuffling, or Macron moving faster toward an exit strategy.
The key question is not whether France can technically pay its bills. The key question is whether investors believe the government has enough political control to make the deficit credible.

Bond markets stay angry unless Paris produces a very clear plan
Expect OAT yields to remain elevated, French spreads over German Bunds to stay wide, and the euro to remain weak unless there is a strong fiscal signal. The market is not asking for perfection. It is asking for credibility.
Next week could include:

More short-term debt issuance because long-term borrowing looks politically and financially risky.
Pressure on French banks as credit risk rises.
More talk about ECB backstop, OMT readiness, or “strategic support,” even if the ECB does not directly buy French bonds.
Possible ugly bond auction if France has to issue medium-term debt in this window.
If the government announces a package that looks like real cuts plus real growth measures, markets may calm slightly. If it looks like political improvisation, yields will stay high and the euro will remain under pressure.

Policing becomes a national flashpoint
The injury to a 15-year-old, the suspension of stun grenades, and the police watchdog investigations mean the government cannot simply repeat last week’s tactics. Next week, police will be more cautious in some places but still confrontational where schools are blockaded or protests turn physical.
Expect:

More official statements about proportionality.
Possible temporary bans on certain equipment in sensitive zones.
More bodycam footage becoming political ammunition.
One high-profile injury case turning into a national symbol.
Mayors and local politicians taking sides, which makes the crisis more territorial.
The government wants to show it is not weak, but it also cannot afford another “hand blown off” moment without a visible response.

Diesel and fuel become a new pressure valve
The decision to release strategic diesel reserves shows the government knows fuel prices can turn an education protest into a broader cost-of-living movement. Next week, pump prices may fall slightly, but that does not guarantee calm.
Possible developments:

Drivers, delivery workers, logistics companies, and local businesses complain about costs or supply organization.
Some trucker or “gilets jaunes”-style mood reappears, especially outside Paris.
The government uses fuel price relief as proof it is acting, while opposition parties say the crisis was created by bad fiscal policy.
This matters because school protests are easier to contain if they stay in schools and streets. If diesel, delivery, transport, and small business join the anger, the government’s room for maneuver shrinks fast.

Marine Le Pen benefits politically, even if she is not yet in government
Le Pen is gaining from two different currents at once:
The street movement gives her anti-establishment credibility.
Her fiscal discipline plan gives her market-friendly credibility that the current government lacks.
Next week expect more Le Pen appearances, economic messaging, and attempts to position herself as the “responsible alternative” while still attacking Macron’s legacy. She will probably not try to look like a radical street leader, but she will also not distance herself from the anger on the ground.

This is dangerous for the current government because it removes their main defensive argument: “the far right is chaotic, we are stable.” If the far right looks fiscally serious and the government looks fiscally weak, the political balance shifts.

The social divide over schools becomes more visible
The headline about wealthy French families leaving state schools shows a deeper story: public education is becoming less universal in practice. Next week expect more arguments about private school vouchers, local school closures, class sizes, teacher shortages, and whether the state can still provide a basic common education.
This will push the government to announce symbolic measures quickly:

More teachers hired temporarily or permanently.
Emergency funding for “priority” schools.
Better security around sensitive institutions.
Promises about exams, support staff, and facilities.
But if those measures are not backed by real money, the protests may simply shift to a new issue.

The political class starts positioning for the next phase
Because Macron is described as outgoing and Le Pen is already being treated as a presidential frontrunner, next week will look less like normal crisis management and more like electioneering under pressure. Édouard Philippe’s campaign activity, LR budget maneuvering in the Senate, and Le Pen’s economic platform all show that people are already thinking beyond Lecornu.
Expect:

More attacks on Macron’s decade in power.
More talk of “renewal,” “responsibility,” and “governance.”
Centrist and conservative factions trying to decide whether to support the government or prepare for its fall.
The left using the student movement to attack austerity.
The far right attacking both fiscal weakness and social disorder.
The next week may not produce a new government, but it will sharpen the question: can Lecornu survive long enough to make the budget credible?

Most likely outcome by the end of the week:

France does not collapse in 7 days, but it becomes more unstable. The government probably survives the immediate budget threat, passes or nearly passes its fiscal plan, and announces emergency education measures. Protests continue but do not fully become a general strike. Bond markets remain nervous. Le Pen gains political ground. The next crisis is already being prepared: either the budget works and the street movement slowly loses momentum, or the budget barely survives and France moves closer to snap election politics, deeper bond-market stress, and more social confrontation.

The biggest risks that would make things worse quickly:

A major protest injury or death.
A failed 49.3 vote or successful no-confidence motion.
An ugly OAT auction.
Diesel shortages or visible fuel price chaos.
A general strike joining the student movement.
ECB being forced to signal active support for French bonds.
The biggest chance of calming things down:

The government passes a credible deficit plan with real education concessions.
Markets see a clear growth-and-savings package, not just cuts.
Police handle demonstrations better than last week.
Le Pen and other opposition leaders cannot unite against the budget.
Fuel prices fall enough to reduce daily-life anger.
One-line prediction: next week France will probably pass its budget by political force or barely avoid a parliamentary blowup, announce emergency school measures, keep hundreds of schools disrupted, and let bond markets decide whether the government is still credible.
