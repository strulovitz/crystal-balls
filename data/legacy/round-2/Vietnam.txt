Based on those headlines, here is my direct, uncensored forecast for Vietnam over the next 7 days. No tables, just plain text.

Short blunt version:
Vietnam will spend the next week looking economically very strong, diplomatically active, and operationally stressed by weather, inflation, and power constraints. The official story will be “we are close to 10% growth,” but the real pressure points will be whether the State Bank can keep inflation from getting ugly, whether banks can successfully raise that $7 billion in capital, whether central Vietnam survives another round of heavy rain/flooding, and whether the US trade deal moves from “largely completed” into something more formal.

1. Central Vietnam flooding will be the most immediate physical story
Likelihood: high.

The AP headline about troops being mobilized after heavy rains, flash floods, and landslide risks in central Vietnam is probably the most urgent item in your list. If Hue and Da Nang are already seeing severe flooding, the next week could bring more rain, rescue operations, evacuations, damaged roads, disrupted ports, and more military involvement.

What I expect to happen:

More flood reports from central coastal provinces and central highlands.
Military units used for rescue, sandbagging, boat transfers, and evacuation support.
Possible school closures, road blockages, airport/port delays in affected areas.
Government officials visiting flooded areas on camera.
Damage assessments starting to come out.
If it gets bad, you may see casualty numbers, landslides, and emergency aid announcements.
This is the kind of story that can dominate daily news even while the economy looks hot. Vietnam can have a 9.95% GDP quarter and still have people in Hue or Da Nang dealing with water up to their knees. That contrast will be very visible.

2. The 10% full-year growth target will become the main economic narrative
Likelihood: very high.

The Q3 GDP number of 9.95% is huge. It puts Vietnam within reach of a 10% full-year growth target, but it also raises the bar for Q4. Officials will not want to admit that the target is fragile, but they also seem aware there are “challenges.”

What I expect to happen:

More official statements saying Vietnam is “optimistic” about hitting 10%.
More emphasis on exports, FDI, manufacturing, and trade surplus.
More pressure on ministries to accelerate project approvals, export promotion, and investment support.
Analysts warning that Q4 must be very strong.
Government pushing consumption, infrastructure spending, and private-sector confidence.
The tone will probably be: “We are doing great, but we must not relax.” That is a classic Vietnamese official style when growth is high but risks are rising.

Blunt take:
The 10% target is now politically important. If Vietnam misses it, the narrative becomes “we had a hot economy but stumbled at the finish line.” So expect extra effort to make Q4 look strong.

3. The State Bank will likely stay cautious on monetary policy
Likelihood: high.

The Reuters headline says inflation is higher than targeted but remains under control. That is an important sentence. It means the central bank does not want to panic, but it also does not want to be seen as loose while prices are rising.

What I expect to happen:

The State Bank will probably keep policy rates steady for now.
More talk about “monitoring inflation,” “liquidity management,” and “stability.”
A cautious tone on the dong, exchange rate, and credit growth.
If inflation keeps rising, they may hint at tighter liquidity or higher short-term rates.
They probably will not do a big dramatic rate hike unless something goes wrong with imported inflation, fuel prices, or food prices.
My prediction:
No major surprise monetary move next week. More likely: steady rates, careful messaging, and open-market operations to manage liquidity. The State Bank wants to support growth without letting inflation get out of hand.

4. Vietnamese banks will keep pushing capital raises
Likelihood: high.

The Reuters headline about banks planning nearly $7 billion in share sales is a big deal. That suggests the banking sector is trying to expand balance sheets, support lending, and position itself for more growth.

What I expect to happen:

More announcements from major Vietnamese banks about capital increases, private placements, or share offerings.
Foreign investor interest being highlighted as a positive signal.
More talk about banks needing capital to support manufacturing, real estate, exports, and infrastructure.
Possible stock market reaction around banking names.
Blunt take:
If Vietnam’s economy is “running red hot,” banks are the transmission mechanism. They need money to lend. The $7 billion plan will probably generate a lot of headlines next week. If one major bank announces a large placement, that could be a significant market event.

More official comments confirming that technical work remains.
Possible joint statement or confirmation of final details.
Export sectors reacting positively: electronics, textiles/apparel, footwear, seafood, coffee, wood products, machinery parts.

Tariff treatment for Vietnamese exports.
Rules of origin, especially for electronics and re-exports.
Whether the US gets stronger access to Vietnamese markets.

The next week may not bring a full signing ceremony, but it will likely bring more official confirmation that the deal is close. If Vietnam announces that technical negotiations are finished, that alone could boost sentiment.

6. Removal from the EU tax haven list will be used as good news for FDI
Likelihood: high for messaging; lower for immediate deals.

The TV5Monde headline says the EU removed Vietnam from its list of tax havens along with Panama. That is a positive compliance signal. It may not create an immediate flood of investment, but it improves Vietnam’s image in European funds, banks, insurance companies, and private equity.

What I expect to happen:

Vietnamese officials or business groups citing the EU decision as proof that reforms are working.
More talk about transparency, tax administration, and investor confidence.
Funds and corporate investors seeing Vietnam as less administratively risky.
Possible follow-up guidance from tax authorities or ministries.
Blunt take:
This is not a “boom tomorrow” headline, but it is useful. It helps Vietnam say: “We are moving toward international standards.” That matters for long-term FDI, especially in banking, insurance, funds, and cross-border investment structures.

7. Power shortages and renewable energy delays will become a bigger issue
Likelihood: medium-high.

Several headlines point to a real tension: Vietnam’s economy is growing fast, exports are booming, factories are expanding, but power shortages and stalled renewable projects are threatening manufacturing competitiveness.

This is one of the most important structural risks in your list.

What I expect to happen:

More discussion about electricity demand, coal imports, gas supply, and renewable project approvals.
Possible announcements on new power projects or energy investments.
Industrial zones being asked to manage demand carefully.
If heat or demand spikes occur, possible load-shedding warnings in some areas.
More pressure on the government to accelerate renewable energy reform.
Blunt take:
Vietnam cannot become a major manufacturing hub if factories keep worrying about electricity. The next week may not bring a full power crisis, but expect more official and private-sector anxiety about energy security. This is one of the main reasons officials are already warning about “challenges” to the 10% growth target.
