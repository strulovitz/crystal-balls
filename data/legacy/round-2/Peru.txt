1. Local election aftermath will dominate domestic politics
This is the most important domestic thread.

The local elections have just happened. The next few days will be about official counting, certification by the JNE, appeals, and legal challenges.

Most likely:

The JNE will certify results for many regional and municipal races.
There will be legal disputes over close districts, especially in Lima, Cusco, Arequipa, Trujillo, Piura, or other major cities.
The Lima mayoral race will generate the most attention because Rafael López Aliaga appears to have won again with around 31% according to exit polls.
Because Bloomberg mentions the ban on immediate reelection, I expect a legal fight over whether his victory is valid if he is considered an incumbent or if the reelection restriction applies.
What that likely means in practice:

If López Aliaga is recognized, his opponents will try to delay installation or force a constitutional review.
If the court questions his eligibility, there could be a runoff, alternate mayor scenario, or temporary administrative confusion.
The polling station fire mentioned by Reuters will probably become part of the legal narrative. Even if it was not “major,” opposition lawyers will ask whether votes from that station need to be audited or recounted.
My prediction:
Lima’s mayoral result will be official but contested. The week will include headlines like “JNE certifies results,” “López Aliaga claims victory,” and “legal challenge filed over reelection ban.” This is very likely.

If the visit falls inside the next 7 days, it becomes the biggest visible story in Peru. If it is slightly later, the next week will still be dominated by final logistics, security drills, route planning, and Vatican-related announcements.

Expect:

Heavy police presence around Lima’s main corridors.
Traffic controls near airports, hotels, churches, and possible papal venues.
A lot of human-interest media about prisoners making souvenirs.
Pilgrims and tourists arriving from other parts of Peru and possibly neighboring countries.
Small protests or counter-events by social groups, indigenous organizations, student movements, or opposition activists trying to use the papal visit as a platform.
The government will want the visit to look peaceful, orderly, and positive because it comes right after local elections and during BTS concerts. So I expect tight crowd management rather than open confrontation.

My prediction:
The papal visit or its final preparation will be a major news item. If the Pope is physically in Peru during this window, expect security headlines, airport arrivals, religious ceremonies, and probably at least one minor protest or traffic disruption. This is very likely to be important.

4. The government will push the “unified command” against crime
DW’s headline about Fujimori announcing a “unified command” against crime is important. That sounds like a political-security package: police, military, intelligence, prosecutors, and maybe regional governments coordinating operations.

In the next week, I expect this to move from announcement to visible action.

Likely developments:

Joint police/military operations in Lima, Callao, Arequipa, Cusco, or other high-crime areas.
Arrests for robbery, narco-trafficking, organized crime, or street gangs.
Seizure of weapons, drugs, vehicles, or cash.
More media coverage of “results” because the government needs quick wins after local elections and before/around the papal visit.
Possible operations in dense urban areas, which can produce dramatic footage but also risk civilian complaints or a viral incident.
The political logic is simple: crime is one of the few issues that crosses party lines. A strong security message helps the government look capable, especially if Congress is fragmented and local elections just produced new mayors/regional presidents who will immediately start blaming the central government for insecurity.

My prediction:
There will be at least one major security operation or arrest package announced in the next week. It may be politically useful even if it is not structurally transformative. Likely.

A push toward security, housing, infrastructure, or economic stability messages.
Attempts to get Congress to cooperate on urgent bills or appointments.
Public calls to “put old political feuds aside,” especially if Keiko Fujimori or another major figure is using that language.
Congressional factions testing whether the cabinet is weak or strong.

My prediction:
The clemency request will become a political debate, but I would not bet on an immediate pardon in the next 7 days. More likely: “the office is reviewing it,” “a legal analysis is being prepared,” or “the matter will be studied with due process.” If a surprise pardon happens, it becomes one of the week’s biggest stories.

An official investigation into the cause.

Firebreak work, road inspections, or safety checks.
Tourism operators adjusting itineraries.
Authorities announcing a prevention plan to avoid looking reactive.

My prediction:
Authorities will announce some safety inspection or restriction related to the Machu Picchu access road. Likely, but not necessarily a full closure.
