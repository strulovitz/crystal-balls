No tables, as requested. Here is the straight, no-sugarcoating forecast for Chile over the next 7 days, based on the headlines you gave me.

Bottom line
Chile is going to look like a country dealing with a weather emergency while a new right-wing government under José Antonio Kast gets its first real stress test. The main story will not be one single explosion, but a mix of: more rain/floods in central Chile and Santiago, political fights over budget cuts, copper/mining problems, inflation worries, and growing tension around the government’s austerity message.

The unvarnished version: no revolution next week, but enough bad news to make the new government look busy, defensive, and politically exposed.

Most likely storylines for the next week
1. Weather will dominate the news cycle — high likelihood, 80%+
This is the obvious one. Santiago has already had torrential rain, a major mudslide in Las Condes, cars swept away, flooded homes and hospitals, residents airlifted to safety, and warnings about possible underground channel blockage. That is not a one-day story. It becomes a week-long story.

What I expect:

More weather warnings for central Chile, especially if El Niño-related rain continues.
Additional flood or landslide alerts in Santiago, eastern hills, and nearby valleys.
More emergency declarations in affected communes.
More rescue operations, helicopter deployments, and cleanup efforts.
Growing media focus on drainage failures, urban planning mistakes, and whether the government was prepared.
Political attacks from the opposition saying the state is unprepared or slow to respond.
Social media outrage, especially if more cars are destroyed, homes damaged, or casualties reported.
The Las Condes angle matters a lot politically because it is an affluent area. That makes the story harder for the government to spin as “only poor areas suffer.” It becomes: even rich neighborhoods are vulnerable, so the infrastructure problem is real and visible.

If another heavy rain event hits in the next few days, expect the next week’s headlines to look like:

“More floods hit Santiago after new rainstorm”
“Government expands disaster zone as Mapocho River remains swollen”
“Kast government announces emergency funds for flood victims”
“Cleanup continues after mudslide destroys vehicles and homes in Las Condes”
This is the most likely main news thread.

2. Kast’s government gets its first major credibility test — high likelihood, 75%
The headlines show a fresh right-wing shift taking power, with budget cuts already being proposed for 2027. That means the government will immediately face the classic problem: austerity is politically painful when people are dealing with floods, inflation, and economic weakness.

Expect the next week to be framed around this tension:

Kast’s government wants to show fiscal discipline.
The public sees rain, mud, damaged infrastructure, and rising fuel costs.
The opposition attacks cuts as broken promises.
The center-left uses programs like “Yo Elijo mi PC” and “Streets Without Violence” to say the right is rolling back social benefits.
The “Yo Elijo mi PC” issue is especially useful for opponents because it was a campaign promise from Kast’s 2021 run. That gives the opposition a simple, emotional narrative: the government is cutting a program it once promised.

Likely developments:

More public criticism from former Boric-era figures and center-left politicians.
Education groups, parents, and students complaining about the PC program cuts.
Security advocates debating whether ending “Streets Without Violence” weakens public safety.
Kast’s government defending the budget as necessary for debt control and long-term stability.
Possible announcements of emergency aid for flood victims, which could create a political dilemma: how do you cut spending while also spending more on disaster response?
This will not necessarily become a mass movement next week, but it will become a constant background fight.

3. Budget and security reform will drive congressional politics — high likelihood, 70%
The headlines mention the 2027 budget process, cuts to social programs, and a new constitutional reform bill on security. That means Congress is going to be active next week.

Expect:

Committee work on the 2027 budget.
Public hearings or debates around spending cuts.
More detail on the security reform bill.
Republican Party figures like Arturo Squella explaining that the reform does not include a new state of emergency, but still changes the security framework.
Opposition attempts to block, delay, or water down parts of the reform.
The security file is politically delicate. The right wants stronger tools; the left worries about police powers and civil liberties. If the bill becomes too aggressive, it can generate protests from police unions, community groups, human rights organizations, and student movements.

My prediction: the next week will be more congressional maneuvering than street explosion, unless the budget cuts hit something very visible like education, health, or pensions.

4. The Centinela copper mine strike will keep supply worries alive — high likelihood, 65%
A strike at a major copper mine after failed talks is already a market story. Add to that Codelco probing possible overstated copper output, and Chile’s mining sector becomes a source of anxiety for investors.

What I expect:

The Centinela strike continues at least into the next week.
More statements from unions and Antofagasta Minerals.
Possible renewed negotiations, but not necessarily an immediate deal.
Market commentary linking the strike to global copper supply concerns.
More scrutiny of Codelco’s production figures if the double-counting probe produces any update.
This matters because Chile is still a major copper producer, and copper is one of the country’s macroeconomic pillars. If markets start questioning whether state miner output was overstated in 2024 and 2025, that becomes more than a technical accounting issue. It becomes a confidence issue.

Possible next-week headlines:

“Centinela strike enters second week as talks stall”
“Codelco probe raises fresh questions over copper output data”
“Copper price wobbles after Chilean mine labor dispute”
“Mining unions demand better terms as production losses grow”
If the Codelco probe finds material overstatement, that could become a mini-crisis for the government’s economic credibility. If not, it remains an uncomfortable background story.

5. Inflation and the central bank will stay in the news — medium-high likelihood, 60%
The headlines show consumer prices rising modestly but still above comfort, fuel costs increasing, and the finance minister admitting that hitting the 3.8% inflation forecast is “challenging.” That gives the central bank a lot of room to sound cautious.

Expect:

More commentary from central bankers about inflation risks.
No dramatic policy surprise unless new data forces their hand.
Political pressure if fuel prices keep rising, because that affects transport, food logistics, and public mood.
The government trying to say the economy is stabilizing while also acknowledging weakness.
The tricky part is that Chile has three bad macro signals at once:

Growth forecast cut to 0.7%.
Inflation still “challenging.”
Mining production down sharply in August.
That combination makes it hard for the government to sound overly optimistic. It also makes the central bank’s job harder because it cannot simply point to strong growth or weak demand.

My prediction: no panic, but more nervous talk about rates, fuel costs, and the inflation target.

The headline about diplomats questioning Gabriel Zaliasnik’s continued presence in Israel while listed as an accused party is the kind of story that can grow if the government handles it badly. It is not yet a full crisis, but it has all the ingredients:

A senior diplomat.
An overseas posting.
Legal controversy.
Institutional pressure from career diplomats.
Opposition interest in using it against the government.
What could happen next week:

More questions in Congress.
Media interviews with diplomats or opposition figures.
The Foreign Ministry issuing a defensive statement.
Kast’s team either defending Zaliasnik, promising review, or hinting at a possible change if legal clarity does not arrive soon.
I would not bet on an immediate resignation or dramatic diplomatic crisis next week. But it is the kind of story that can keep bubbling and become a recurring irritation.

7. Mapuche tensions may flare again — medium likelihood, 35%
The DW piece on the Mapuche “between resistance and change” suggests ongoing land, identity, and governance friction. That does not mean a major national crisis next week, but it does mean there is enough underlying tension that one incident can quickly become news.

Possible developments:

Protests in Araucanía or other southern regions.
Road blocks or community assemblies.
Police-community clashes if security forces respond heavily.
International media interest if the government uses the security reform narrative to justify stronger actions.
If the new security reform bill becomes more aggressive, Mapuche communities may see it as another reason to push back. That could turn a regional issue into a national political story.

8. Migration and labor market narratives will matter — medium likelihood, 40%
The Bloomberg headline about Venezuelans leaving Chile is interesting because it touches on two sensitive topics at once:

Labor market pressure.
Immigration politics.
If foreign unemployment is falling because Venezuelans are leaving, while local joblessness remains high, the government can use that to say the labor market is adjusting. The opposition can counter that locals are still suffering and that migration policy needs clearer direction.

Expect:

More discussion about foreign workers leaving Chile.
Employer complaints in sectors that relied on migrant labor.
Right-wing talk about order, regularization, and border management.
Left-wing concerns about deportations, informal work, and integration.
This will not be the biggest story next week, but it fits the new government’s likely political brand: more emphasis on order, fiscal control, and labor market discipline.

Criticism from cultural figures if budget cuts affect arts or education programs.
Some international media coverage of Chile as a literary/cultural destination.
This is not a crisis area, but it can become part of the broader “right-wing government cutting culture” narrative.
