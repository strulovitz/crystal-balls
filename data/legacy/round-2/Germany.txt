No tables, as requested. Here is my straight forecast for Germany over the next week, based on the headlines you gave me.

Overall picture: Germany will not have a full-blown crisis in the next seven days, but it will feel politically tense, security-minded, and economically uneasy. The country will be dealing with three big shocks at once: a spy/treason scandal involving a former BND chief, the far-right AfD becoming more normalized in state politics, and Chancellor Merz trying to hold his coalition together while pushing hard on Ukraine, defense, and trade policy against China.

Most likely things that will happen next week:

The Hanning espionage case will dominate Berlin politics
This is going to be the emotional center of the week. A former head of Germany’s foreign intelligence service being arrested for suspected espionage and attempted treason is a big deal in Germany, where the BND is usually treated as a somewhat sacred institution.
I expect:

more court proceedings or detention reviews around August Hanning
parliamentary questions from opposition parties
demands for a special committee, inquiry, or security review
intense media digging into who else was involved, what secrets were shared, and which foreign power benefited
pressure on the current intelligence chief to explain how this happened
CDU/SPD/Green/FDP/SWLinstrumental fights over whether this is a government failure, an opposition success story, or proof that Russia is getting closer to German decision-making
If the case starts looking like it involves Russia, Merz will probably use it to justify stronger defense spending, tighter security checks, and a harder line on hybrid warfare. If it looks more like internal corruption or careerism, the opposition will use it to attack the government’s competence.

Either way: scandal politics.

The AfD’s Saxony-Anhalt breakthrough will become a tactical battle
The fact that the AfD has now secured the speaker role in a state parliament for the first time since 1945 is politically significant. It is not just a symbolic win. It gives the AfD more access, more legitimacy, and more leverage inside state institutions.
Next week I expect:

the new AfD speaker to make statements that sound calm, institutional, and “normal”
established parties in Saxony-Anhalt arguing over how to deal with them
debates about whether the AfD can be included in government negotiations or should be kept out of key committees
protests against the AfD, especially from left-wing groups, unions, churches, and local civic organizations
counter-protests or pro-AfD events, particularly in eastern Germany
Berlin parties using the case to attack each other: the CDU saying “we must stop them,” the SPD/Greens saying “you made this possible,” the AfD saying “the establishment is panicking”
This will feed directly into Merz’s problem. His own party is being pushed from the right while his coalition partners push from the left and center-left.

Merz’s coalition will be under serious pressure
Merz’s approval rating has dropped, and the CDU has suffered losses. That matters because his government depends on a fragile balance between CDU/CSU, SPD, and Greens.
Next week I expect:

intense internal coalition talks
fights over pension reform, healthcare costs, budget rules, energy relief, defense spending, or some combination of those
SPD and Green demands for visible concessions to their voters
CDU pressure from its base not to give too much away
possible ministerial reshuffling or at least rumors of one
Merz trying to frame the talks as “responsible reform” rather than “crisis management”
My prediction: there will be a narrow compromise, but it will not satisfy everyone. It will probably look like a package that gives each coalition partner something small enough to claim victory while leaving the deeper structural problems unresolved.

If the talks go badly, Merz may delay decisions and push the issue into next month, because in Germany governments rarely collapse over one reform fight unless the damage becomes public and ugly.

The economy will give mixed signals
The economic picture is not “boom” and not “collapse.” It is messy.
From your headlines:

services sector doing better than expected
industrial output rebounding
factory orders falling sharply, especially in capital goods like aircraft, ships, trains, and military vehicles
government raising growth forecasts for 2026 and 2027
businesses still under pressure from weak demand, energy costs, and global uncertainty
So next week I expect:

more economic data that confuses the market
industrial data looking better in some areas but weaker in others
continued debate about whether Germany is stabilizing or just getting a temporary rebound
Bund yields staying relatively attractive as investors seek safety
companies like VW, Porsche, and BMW making strategic announcements that reflect cost pressure, restructuring, and a shift toward higher-margin products
The auto industry will be especially important. If Volkswagen ends collective wage agreements and labor disputes heat up, that could become a visible social-economic story. I would not be surprised if union tensions or works council disputes get more attention in the coming days.

Germany will keep pushing harder on Ukraine and defense
Merz’s Kyiv visit, the new aid package, and the intelligence warnings about Russia make this a central theme.
I expect next week:

follow-up announcements on German military support for Ukraine
more talk about drones, missiles, air defense, and naval procurement
political debate in the Bundestag about how much Germany should invest in defense
intelligence agencies continuing to warn about hybrid threats from Russia
possible cyberattacks or disruptions targeting German infrastructure, energy systems, ports, telecoms, or data centers
Merz using the Hanning case and the “shadow war” language to argue that Germany must become more security-conscious
This does not mean open war next week. But it does mean Germany will sound more like a country preparing for prolonged conflict with Russia than a country trying to return to normal.

The China/EU trade fight will intensify
Germany blocking the Cosco-related logistics deal and France/Germany pushing a new EU trade weapon show that Berlin is getting much harder on China.
Next week I expect:

more debate in Brussels about rare earths, magnets, critical supplies, and market access
Chinese diplomatic or commercial pushback
German industry lobbying hard against measures that could hurt carmakers, exporters, and manufacturers
public arguments over whether Germany is finally standing up to China or just making a political gesture
possible new screening rules, state intervention in deals, or calls for a “second-strike” tool to cut off strategic suppliers
This is one of the bigger shifts in German politics: the old idea that trade with China is always good is weakening fast.

Energy costs and transport politics will stay hot
Eurozone inflation has risen because of energy costs, diesel prices are high, and Germany has already moved on fuel tax relief and public transport pricing.
I expect:

continued public frustration over fuel, heating, and transport costs
possible government announcements about emergency diesel reserves, price support, or further relief measures
more debate over whether the Deutschlandticket price increase is politically acceptable
Deutsche Bahn using AI/modernization stories to improve its image after years of punctuality problems
This will not be the biggest story, but it will matter for voter mood, especially among commuters, small businesses, and rural voters.

Migration politics will become more visible
The Rwanda/EU migration negotiations and the AfD’s strength in eastern Germany suggest that migration is becoming a more emotional issue again.
Next week I expect:

more protests around migration policy
local debates in eastern German states about integration, housing, border controls, and asylum processing
government attempts to present migration policy as “realistic” rather than “crisis-driven”
opposition parties using migration to attack the coalition from both sides
This will not be as dominant as the spy scandal or AfD state politics, but it will keep feeding the political mood.

Less likely but possible in the next week:

A cabinet reshuffle if Merz needs to reset his team after the CDU losses and falling approval ratings
A visible Russian hybrid incident, such as a cyberattack on energy, transport, or government systems
A stronger labor dispute at Volkswagen or another major employer
More arrests or search warrants in the Hanning case
A formal German contribution to an EU emergency diesel stock release
Increased talk of early federal elections if coalition tensions get very ugly
A court ruling that changes the legal status of the former spy chief
A new German-EU trade statement against China
What I think is most important to watch:

The Hanning case will be the story people remember.
The AfD’s Saxony-Anhalt role will be the political shockwave.
Merz’s coalition talks will determine whether he looks strong or weakened.
The economic data will show that Germany is not in free fall, but it is not healthy either.
The Ukraine/China/defense line will show that Germany is becoming more assertive and more security-focused.

My bottom line:
Next week, Germany will look like a country under pressure but still functioning. Merz will be trying to project strength, but the political environment is tightening around him. The AfD is becoming harder to ignore. The intelligence scandal will make Berlin nervous. The economy will send mixed messages. And the government will keep pushing a harder line on Russia, Ukraine, defense, and China.

In plain terms: no collapse, but more tension, more security talk, more far-right momentum, and more economic anxiety.
