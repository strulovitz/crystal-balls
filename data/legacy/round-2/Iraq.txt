celebrating the US withdrawal
keeping oil moving despite Hormuz pressure

Most likely 7-day outcome
Iraq will enter the next week in a “controlled celebration” mode. The US is gone, so Baghdad will push the narrative hard: sovereignty restored, independence affirmed, no more foreign boots on the ground. But the withdrawal does not remove Iraq’s problems. It removes one layer of external pressure and replaces it with a bigger Iranian opening.

So expect:

Baghdad to stay politically loud but militarily cautious
Iranian-backed groups to gain confidence without immediately going reckless
Kurdistan to face a real security-management problem after the US exit
the economy to become the biggest domestic pain point
oil exports to remain the center of gravity for Baghdad’s survival
What I expect to happen in Iraq next week
1. The withdrawal celebration will continue, but the mood will shift quickly from triumph to “what now?”
The first part of the week will still be dominated by Sovereignty Day imagery: flags, parades, speeches, photos of the last handover. The prime minister and security officials will use this heavily to claim stability and national pride.

But within a few days, the conversation will shift toward practical problems:

Who guarantees security now?
What happens in Kirkuk, Sulaymaniyah, Erbil, and the border areas?
How does Baghdad keep paying salaries if oil revenue is squeezed?
Why did the dinar fall after the “victory”?
That last point will be politically awkward. A government that just celebrated independence and sovereignty may find itself facing public anger over currency weakness, higher import prices, and possible salary delays.

My call: the celebration lasts publicly for a few days, then the political tone becomes more defensive as economic complaints grow.

2. Iran will move in fast, but mostly through politics, religion, logistics, and militias — not tanks
The headlines make this clear: Iran is treating the US exit as a win. The next week will likely show that in several ways:

More Iranian flights to Najaf
More Iranian clerical and political activity around Najaf/Karbala
Louder rhetoric from Iraqi Shia militias
Greater pressure on Baghdad to grant waivers, overflight rights, or transit cooperation
A visible attempt by Tehran to make Iraq feel more connected to Iran’s regional orbit
The 40 daily Iranian flights to Najaf are not just a transport issue. They are a symbolic and economic signal. Najaf is holy, political, and commercially important. If Iran can keep those flights running, it gets:

religious tourism flowing
economic activity in southern Iraq
more contact with Iraqi clerics and politicians
a visible sign that the US exit has not cut Iraq off from Tehran
My call: expect Baghdad to accept more Iranian pressure next week, especially around Najaf and sanctions-related waivers. It will probably try to present this as normal bilateral cooperation rather than “Iran coming back in.”

3. The dinar devaluation will be the biggest domestic irritant
This is one of the most important items in your list. Moving the official rate from 1,300 to 1,500 IQD/USD is a big deal. That is not a small technical adjustment. It tells people:

the state is under fiscal pressure
oil revenue is not coming in as easily as before
imports will become more expensive
public-sector salaries may feel weaker in real terms
the parallel market may keep testing even higher levels
In Iraq, currency weakness does not stay an economic story for long. It quickly becomes:

merchant protests
public-sector anger
complaints about delayed or reduced salaries
blame directed at the government and oil ministry
accusations that “the withdrawal was a victory on paper but a loss in people’s pockets”
My call: next week will likely bring more public frustration over the dinar, especially in Basra, Najaf, Karbala, and other oil-rich or Shia-majority areas. Not necessarily a huge nationwide uprising, but enough street-level anger to make politicians nervous.

4. Oil will be the real center of gravity — not just economics, but politics and security
Several headlines point to the same thing: Iraq is trying hard to keep crude moving despite Hormuz disruption, Iranian pressure, and deeper discounts.

That means next week Baghdad will likely focus on:

arranging more tankers
pushing Basrah Medium exports forward
deepening discounts to keep Chinese and Indian buyers interested
announcing successful transits through or around Hormuz
trying to show the world that Iraq’s oil is still reliable even if the region is unstable
This matters because Iraq’s government lives on oil money. If exports slow, revenue falls, salaries get late, subsidies weaken, and political coalitions start snapping at each other.

So expect Baghdad to make oil logistics a national priority. You will probably see more statements like:

“Another successful tanker transit”
“Iraq secures additional vessels”
“October/November cargoes sold despite discounts”
“Baghdad works to protect export routes”
My call: the next week will likely include at least one high-profile Iraqi oil-logistics announcement, probably tied to a tanker moving 2 million barrels or similar. It will be framed as a success, even if the revenue per barrel is worse than before.

5. The government will push its $2.7 billion stimulus package, but it may not calm everyone quickly
The approved financing package is important because Baghdad knows the devaluation and oil discounts are going to hurt. The government needs to show it is doing something.

So next week expect:

more details on how the 3.5 trillion dinar package will be spent
announcements aimed at calming merchants, workers, and public-sector employees
attempts to frame the package as “stabilization” and “growth support”
political bargaining over who gets what from it
But here is the blunt part: a few billion dollars does not instantly fix a confidence crisis. If people feel their salaries are weaker or delayed, they will still complain. The package may help prevent things from getting much worse, but it will not erase the anger.

My call: the government will use the stimulus heavily in its messaging next week, but public frustration will remain visible.

6. Parliament will start getting tangled in the 2027 budget fight
The budget story is very important. A $48.8 billion deficit is not a small problem for an oil-dependent state already dealing with weaker currency and discounted crude prices.

Next week, as parliamentary discussions begin, expect:

fights over oil revenue sharing
Kurdish demands on federal transfers
Shia bloc pressure on salaries and subsidies
debates over spending cuts vs. stimulus
accusations that the government is hiding the true fiscal damage
attempts by the prime minister to present unity while coalitions bargain behind the scenes
This will not necessarily produce an immediate crisis, but it will make the political environment noisier. Budget fights in Iraq often become identity fights: center vs. periphery, Shia vs. Sunni vs. Kurd, Baghdad vs. Basra, Baghdad vs. Erbil.

My call: expect more political bickering next week as the budget process opens. No immediate collapse, but a lot of pressure on the ruling coalition.

7. Kurdistan will be one of the most sensitive security spots after the US exit
The headlines about a “security vacuum” in the Kurdistan region are significant. When the US leaves, it is not just a symbolic change. It affects:

command and control coordination
intelligence sharing
deterrence against local armed groups
border management
federal-Kurdish friction points
militia activity near Kirkuk and Sulaymaniyah
The Peshmerga unification story is interesting because it suggests the Kurdish region wants to look more organized and national after the US departure. But in practice, that process is messy. Different parties still control forces, and local commanders still matter.

My call: next week will likely bring at least one minor security incident or tense episode in Kurdistan or near the federal-Kurdish border areas. It could be:

a checkpoint standoff
a small exchange of fire
a drone alert
a militia clash
a disputed road or oil facility issue
It probably will not become a full war next week, but it is the kind of incident that can make headlines and raise fears.

8. Shia militias will get bolder in tone, but Baghdad will try to keep them on a leash
With the US gone, Iraqi militias have more room to act politically and rhetorically. But they also know that if they become too aggressive too soon, they can:

provoke federal forces
scare investors
create friction with Kurds or Sunnis
give Baghdad an excuse to clamp down
So I expect a mix of:

stronger public statements
more visible presence in political life
pressure on the government over Iran, Hormuz, and US sanctions
occasional small demonstrations or ceremonies
but no full-scale armed takeover next week
The militias will probably try to position themselves as the “real guarantors” of sovereignty now that the US is gone. That means more media visibility and more political weight in Baghdad.

My call: expect louder militia voices next week, especially around Najaf, Karbala, and southern Iraq, but still enough restraint to avoid an immediate nationwide security crisis.

9. The “secret Israeli base in Iraq” story could become a domestic political weapon
This is one of the more potentially explosive items in your list. If reports that Israel operated a clandestine base in Iraq during the war gain traction, Iraqi opposition figures will not let it go quietly.

Expect:

questions in parliament
accusations against the government for allowing or failing to detect it
demands for an inquiry
possible protests, especially from Shia and nationalist factions
Kurdish embarrassment if the base was in their region
attempts by Baghdad to downplay, deny, or call it temporary/limited
This story is politically useful because it lets opponents attack the government on two fronts at once:

“You claimed sovereignty, but foreigners were still operating here”
“You are either too close to Iran or too weak to control your own territory”
My call: this will not necessarily dominate the whole week, but it will likely become a recurring political irritant. If it spreads, expect more opposition pressure and possibly a parliamentary demand for clarification.

10. The US will be gone from the ground, but not gone from the region
This is crucial. The withdrawal does not mean the US disappears from Iraqi affairs. It just changes its posture.

Given the other headlines — carrier groups in the region, naval protection for tankers, US bombers being pulled from Britain after a suspicious incident, and an active Iran conflict — the next week will likely still show:

US naval presence near Hormuz
possible air patrols or overflight activity
American statements about protecting shipping
Baghdad trying to maintain official neutrality while privately coordinating with Washington
If something happens in Hormuz, the US may strike or threaten to strike without returning troops to Iraq. That means Iraq can still feel the conflict even without a ground mission.

My call: expect the US to remain a major offshore factor next week. If shipping incidents continue, Baghdad will be forced to manage its relationship with both Washington and Tehran more carefully than before.

Bottom line, uncensored

The US exit gives Baghdad a political victory, but it hands Tehran a bigger opening and leaves Iraq more exposed to the Iran-US fight over Hormuz. The government’s biggest problem next week will not be one single battle. It will be managing money, militias, Kurds, and public anger at the same time.

If I had to summarize it in one sentence:

Iraq will spend the next week trying to look sovereign on the outside while struggling with Iranian pressure, oil stress, currency pain, and militia politics on the inside.
