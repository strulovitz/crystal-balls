My base case for China over the next seven days — roughly October 4 to October 10, 2026 — is that Beijing will try to turn the recent Xi-Trump summit into a domestic confidence story while pushing small but visible economic support measures, starting implementation of the US-China tariff cuts, testing the United States on chips and Taiwan, and preparing a calibrated response if the EU moves against Chinese trade.

In plain terms: China will likely spend the next week managing the narrative that the economy is stabilizing, the property market is getting help, the US relationship is improving, and China is winning “face” abroad — even though the underlying economic problems are still serious.

Here is what I expect.

MOST LIKELY OUTCOME FOR THE NEXT WEEK

China will use National Day as a propaganda and consumption push
The country is in or just finishing its Golden Week holiday. Expect state media to lean heavily on National Day imagery: fireworks, travel crowds, pandas, military strength, AI achievements, and the idea that Xi’s Washington visit brought respect.

The official story will probably be:

China is stable.
Confidence is returning.
The US-China relationship is more mature.
Foreign leaders are showing respect to Beijing.
Consumers are spending again.
Technology is advancing.
The property sector is being supported.
But the real domestic mood is likely mixed. People are patriotic, but they are still worried about jobs, housing prices, youth unemployment, household debt, and whether the economy will actually improve. So expect a public display of confidence with private skepticism underneath.

Local governments will probably announce more property and consumption support
The September rate cut and mortgage subsidies are important, but they will not be left alone. I expect local governments to use the post-holiday period to push additional measures, especially in major cities where property sales matter a lot for confidence.

Likely actions include:

lower mortgage rates for first-home buyers;
reduced down-payment requirements;
purchase vouchers or rebates for homes, cars, appliances, or electronics;
local government buyback programs for unsold housing inventory;
faster processing of property transactions;
marketing campaigns aimed at making home-buying feel safer.
This is not likely to be a giant national stimulus package yet. The bigger policy meeting usually comes later in October. But the next week may see enough city-level and ministry-level moves to keep the “policy support” story alive.

The goal is simple: make Q4 look better, protect the GDP target, and avoid the impression that the property slump is getting out of control.

Holiday consumption data will be spun as a recovery sign
After the holiday, officials will release travel, tourism, retail, cinema, restaurant, and transport numbers. The official framing will likely emphasize growth.

But I would expect the real picture to be more complicated:

Travel volumes may look good.
People may still spend carefully.
Discounts may be deeper than normal.
Luxury spending may remain soft.
Property sales may improve slightly in some cities but not broadly.
Service consumption may be better than goods consumption.
Companies will likely emphasize “high-quality consumption” and domestic brands.
State media will probably highlight positive examples: crowded airports, popular tourist sites, restaurant queues, panda visits, AI-powered attractions, and National Day events. The message will be that consumer life is returning to normal.

A-shares may reopen with some optimism, but not a wild rally
Chinese markets will likely reopen after the holiday around October 8. If there is no major overnight shock, I would expect the market to open cautiously positive or modestly higher.

The main supportive factors are:

the US-China tariff-cut lists;
property support measures;
rate cut momentum;
AI and tech optimism;
the post-summit sense that Beijing has some diplomatic “face”;
possible holiday consumption data.
Sectors likely to attract attention:

AI chips and software;
robotics;
batteries and electric vehicles;
semiconductors;
consumer electronics;
property developers, but only if new support measures appear;
tourism, airlines, hotels, and retail if holiday data look strong.
But I would not expect a euphoric rally. The weak industrial profit growth, consumer stock weakness, and still uncertain global trade environment will cap enthusiasm. The market may rise on policy hope, but investors will want proof that demand is actually improving.

If EU-China trade friction or Taiwan-related military activity escalates during the week, volatility could increase.

The US-China tariff-cut deal will start to look real
The $60 billion tariff-cut product lists are probably the most useful concrete outcome of the Trump-Xi summit. Next week, I expect working-level implementation to begin or accelerate.

That may include:

customs guidance;
industry association briefings;
company-level discussions on which products qualify;
announcements about US farm goods, toys, sports equipment, Christmas decorations, electronics, and other listed items;
Chinese statements praising “pragmatic cooperation” with the United States.
Beijing will want to show that the summit produced results, not just photos. The pandas were symbolic; the tariff lists are economic. Expect Chinese officials to highlight specific companies or products benefiting from lower tariffs.

But this is still a truce, not a full normalization. The bigger issues remain unresolved:

Taiwan;
chips;
rare earths and critical minerals;
AI competition;
tech investment rules;
overcapacity;
industrial subsidies;
cyber espionage.
So the next week will probably show cooperation on trade lists while still having friction underneath.

China may allow some Nvidia chip purchases, but selectively
The reports that China may let companies such as Alibaba and ByteDance buy certain Nvidia chips are important. I think Beijing is trying to balance two goals:

keep its biggest AI players supplied;
avoid looking too dependent on the United States.
So my prediction is selective permission, not a full open gate.

Possible outcomes next week:

Chinese tech firms quietly begin procurement discussions;
regulators issue end-use or data-security conditions;
state media frames it as “pragmatic” rather than “dependent”;
domestic chip companies get political support to keep pushing localization;
officials avoid making it sound like a major win for Nvidia.
If China does allow these purchases, the market reaction in AI hardware, cloud computing, and large language model companies would likely be positive. But Beijing will probably manage the narrative carefully so it does not look like it is rewarding US chipmakers too much.

The EU will be a flashpoint if it moves against Chinese trade
China has already warned that it will respond “resolutely” if the EU introduces restrictions targeting Chinese businesses or products. I expect this to become more active in the next week, especially around G20 trade tensions and overcapacity complaints.

Likely Chinese responses:

Ministry of Commerce statements;
requests for consultations;
industry dialogues;
possible countermeasure lists;
targeted pressure on politically sensitive EU products;
use of rare earths or critical minerals as leverage if needed.
But I would not expect China to launch a full-scale trade war against Europe in the next seven days. Beijing probably wants to keep the US relationship calmer while still showing strength against Europe.

Possible targets for Chinese pressure could include:

EU agricultural products;
wine or dairy;
cars or EVs;
machinery or chemicals;
specific companies with high political visibility.
The most likely style is calibrated retaliation: enough to hurt, not enough to break the relationship. Spain’s closer ties to China may also complicate EU unity, so Beijing may try to exploit divisions inside Europe.

Taiwan will remain a military and propaganda focus
The arrival of F-16V jets in Taiwan is significant, but I do not expect a major crisis next week. The more likely pattern is:

sharper Chinese rhetoric around National Day;
PLA exercises near or around Taiwan;
more aircraft or ship activity in the Taiwan Strait;
Chinese state media emphasizing that Taiwan is a core issue;
US officials quietly monitoring but avoiding a big public escalation.
The US view, according to the Reuters headline, is that an invasion before 2028 is increasingly unlikely. That reduces the chance of a sudden major military move in the next week. But China will still use the period to show strength.

I would expect routine-to-elevated tensions, not a full incident — unless there is an accidental clash or a politically symbolic over-the-top exercise. The risk is higher than normal because Taiwan just received new US-made jets and because National Day gives Beijing a reason to posture.

AI will remain the center of China’s domestic tech story
The headlines show that AI is everywhere: factory expansion, commodity imports, talent restrictions, bioweapons concerns, relationship apps, chip purchases, robot IPO rules, and US-China dialogue. Next week, I expect AI to dominate both policy and market attention.

Possible developments:

tighter regulation of AI companion apps;
more scrutiny of data collection and user privacy;
safety reviews for large models after the Kimi bioweapons story;
technical talks under the new US-China AI incident channel;
continued travel restrictions on top AI talent and their families;
government support for domestic model developers;
state media promoting “AI for daily life” stories.
China wants to appear both open and controlled: open enough to keep its companies competitive, controlled enough to prevent chaos or leakage of strategic advantage. The crackdown on AI relationships is a good example. It is not just about romance apps; it is about data, youth behavior, consumer spending, and platform power.

China will continue using “face” as an economic asset
One of the clearest themes in the headlines is that Beijing is enjoying the diplomatic respect from Trump. The FT headline “Xi got face” captures this well. For China, this matters because domestic confidence is tied to international standing.

Next week, state media and official commentary will likely emphasize:

Xi was welcomed as an equal;
Trump showed respect;
China gained dignity without losing core interests;
the pandas are a sign of friendship;
the tariff cuts show cooperation;
the US is taking China seriously.
This “face” narrative helps domestic policy. If China looks strong abroad, it is easier for officials to ask people and companies to accept continued effort at home: save more, invest in tech, buy homes with subsidies, support state priorities, and tolerate short-term pain.

But this also creates pressure. Once Beijing claims the summit was a success, it has to show results. If the economy weakens again or trade fights intensify, the gap between official confidence and private reality could become more visible.

If you want to track what is actually happening in China over the next week, watch these items:

Property announcements from major cities, especially tier-1 and strong tier-2 cities.
PBOC or banking regulator guidance on mortgage subsidies.
Ministry of Commerce statements about the EU and US tariff lists.
Any official confirmation that Alibaba, ByteDance, or other firms can buy Nvidia chips.
PLA exercises around Taiwan.
Holiday consumption and tourism data released after Golden Week.
A-share market reaction when trading resumes.
Rare earth export policy signals.
AI regulation news, especially around companion apps, data, and model safety.
Social media mood on housing, youth anxiety, jobs, and tax pressure.

WHAT WOULD SURPRISE ME

The biggest surprises would be:

A major national stimulus package before the late-October Politburo meeting
That would be possible if officials are very worried about Q4 momentum, but the current “mini stimulus” tone suggests they want to save the bigger tools for later.
A full EU-China trade escalation in one week
China could react strongly, but I think it will prefer a staged response unless Europe announces something very aggressive.
A Taiwan incident
Not an invasion, but a close encounter, live-fire exercise near the median line, or dramatic military show that forces both Washington and Beijing to manage the situation quickly.
China suddenly restricting Nvidia chips again after signaling openness
That would suggest Beijing is more worried about domestic politics than about keeping its AI companies supplied.
A visible consumer slump in holiday data
If travel and spending numbers look weak enough to undermine the official narrative, expect faster policy action and more aggressive state media framing.
BOTTOM LINE

My main prediction is that China’s next week will be a managed stabilization week.

Beijing will likely:

push property and consumption support;
use National Day for confidence-building;
implement or advertise US-China tariff-cut progress;
allow selective chip access while keeping leverage;
posture around Taiwan without starting a crisis;
prepare a measured response to EU trade pressure;
tighten AI regulation where politically sensitive;
spin holiday data as evidence of recovery.
The most important thing to understand is that China is trying to convert diplomatic “face” into economic confidence. The Trump-Xi summit gave Beijing a useful story: the United States is taking China seriously, trade can be managed, and China can get respect without giving up its core position. Next week will probably be about making that story feel real through policy details, market signals, consumption data, and controlled geopolitical posturing.

The risk is that the private sector and ordinary households still feel the pain: weak incomes, falling housing prices, youth job pressure, tax scrutiny, and uncertainty about foreign investment. So the official optimism will have to be backed by visible measures. If those measures appear, confidence may improve modestly. If they do not, the gap between state narrative and lived experience will widen.
